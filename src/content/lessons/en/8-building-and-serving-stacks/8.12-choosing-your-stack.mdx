You have met eleven ways to run the same model. Somebody now asks you which one to use, and the honest first move is to refuse the question as posed.

## Why "which stack is fastest?" has no answer

There is no fastest stack, because speed is not a property of a stack. It is a property of the pairing of a stack with a model, a piece of hardware, and a traffic pattern. Change any of the three and the ranking changes. What you can have instead is a procedure that reaches a defensible answer in three questions, plus a standard for what counts as evidence.

## Does the model fit your hardware?

The first question is **hardware**, because it is the only one that can end the conversation immediately.

- Qwen3.8-27B in bf16 is 54 GB of weights before a single token of cache. That means an 80 GB-class accelerator, a multi-GPU shard, or a very large unified-memory machine.
- The pinned Q4_K_M text artifact is 17.1 GB (15.93 GiB). That opens a 24 GB card, a 32 GB Mac, and a well-specified laptop, after runtime headroom is validated.
- More aggressive supported quants can be smaller. Choose a smaller model only when no quant with acceptable quality, support, and latency fits.

If your memory cannot hold the weights plus the cache your context length demands, no engine choice rescues you.

## What shape is the traffic, and who keeps it running?

The second question is **workload shape**, and it has more dimensions than people expect:

- Is traffic one person at a keyboard, or hundreds of concurrent sessions?
- Do requests share a long identical prefix (a system prompt, a policy document, a few-shot preamble), or is every prompt unique?
- Is the context two thousand tokens or two hundred thousand?
- Must output conform to a schema?
- Are images involved?
- Is thinking mode on, so generations are long?

Each of these pushes toward a different engine, and they can conflict. That is why they are answered before a stack is named rather than after.

The third question is **ops capacity**, and it is the one engineers systematically under-weight. Somebody upgrades drivers. Somebody notices the p99 latency (the latency that 99 percent of requests beat) drifting. Somebody is awake when a kernel regression lands. A stack that is ten percent faster and consumes a quarter of an engineer is not ten percent better.

## From three answers to a stack

Answer the three questions and a map follows:

- **One machine, one user, Apple silicon:** MLX from [lesson 8.9](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.9-mlx/), or a llama.cpp runner from [lesson 8.8](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.8-ollama-and-local-runners/) if you want the convenience and the registry.
- **One NVIDIA GPU serving an internal API:** vLLM from [lesson 8.4](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.4-vllm/), which is the default and deserves to be.
- **Many concurrent users with heavily shared prefixes, or output that must be valid JSON:** SGLang from [lesson 8.5](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.5-sglang/), whose prefix sharing and constrained decoding solve exactly those two problems.
- **An NVIDIA-only fleet, throughput as the dominant cost, and engineering time to spend on runtime and compatibility validation:** TensorRT-LLM from [lesson 8.6](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.6-tensorrt-llm/).
- **A heterogeneous or future-heterogeneous fleet, with tolerance for a young ecosystem:** MAX from [lesson 8.10](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.10-modular-max-mojo/).
- **Low, spiky, or unpredictable volume with no ops rotation:** the managed endpoint from [lesson 8.11](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.11-serving-in-the-cloud/).

Work one case fully. A team of three engineers serves 200 daily users, mostly short chats at around 8,192 tokens of context, and needs replies as validated JSON. They can fund one 80 GB accelerator.

1. **Hardware.** [Lesson 9.3](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.3-kv-cache-sizing-and-memory-budgets/) does this budget line by line and lands on a 23.3 GiB cache pool once weights, workspace and activations are taken out. Using the complete sizing from lesson 9.3, with [lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/)'s KV-cache rule and the reference Transformers path keeping Gated DeltaNet state in fp32, roughly 36 live 8K sequences fit concurrently. That is far above what 200 users spread across a working day need at once. One card is enough, and they do not need to quantize.
2. **Workload.** The JSON requirement and a shared system prompt point squarely at SGLang. Identical prefixes across users mean the prefill for that prompt is computed once and reused, which is throughput they gain without any tuning.
3. **Ops.** Three engineers building a product is thin cover for a serving deployment. So before committing, they compare against the managed option using lesson 8.11's arithmetic. If their token volume resembles that lesson's example, the metered bill is small enough that owning a card would be buying idle silicon.

Notice what decided it. Not a benchmark. Capacity arithmetic, a feature requirement, and a headcount.

## What makes a comparison fair?

:::warning
This lesson exists to stop one habit: treating "fastest" as transferable. A published tokens-per-second figure encodes a precision, a batch size, an input length, an output length, a hardware SKU (the exact product model), and a software version, and it is valid only inside that box. The most common way engineers fool themselves is comparing a 4-bit deployment on one stack with a bf16 deployment on another and calling the difference a stack difference. It is a precision difference, and precision is a change to the model, not to the serving of it.
:::

So what makes a comparison fair? Six things must be identical across arms (the stacks being compared):

1. the model revision;
2. the numeric precision of both weights and KV cache;
3. the decoding configuration. For this model that means one of the two published presets, since thinking mode at temperature 1.0 generates far more tokens than the instruct preset at 0.7 and will move throughput on its own;
4. the input and output length distributions;
5. the concurrency level and arrival pattern;
6. the measurement window, with a stated warm-up policy.

Then report the right things. Report throughput alongside time-to-first-token and p50 (median) and p99 end-to-end latency, because a stack that wins on aggregate tokens per second while doubling tail latency has lost for an interactive product. And state whether prefix caching was warm, since a benchmark that replays one prompt measures a cache, not a model.

Write the run as a four-part **benchmark passport**:

1. **Identity:** exact model and artifact revision, runtime/build, driver and kernels, template, weight/KV/recurrent-state formats, and speculative decoder.
2. **Protocol:** prompt or benchmark version, harness and scorer revision, sampling and reasoning policy, output budget, input/output distributions, concurrency and arrival pattern, prefix-cache state, warm-up, repetitions, and random seeds where applicable.
3. **Validity:** correctness canaries before timing, configured versus realized capacity, preregistered discard rules, and separate counts for admission rejection, length truncation, schema/tool failure, and completed-but-wrong output.
4. **Results:** TTFT, inter-token or decode rate, end-to-end p50/p95/p99, aggregate throughput, peak memory, errors and restarts, plus wall power or energy only when it was actually measured. Preserve raw requests, responses, and logs whenever licensing and privacy permit.

This structure extends the deployment lesson of the public campaign described below with the same principles used by MLPerf and HELM, two established benchmark suites: define the scenario, standardize the conditions, validate accuracy, expose multiple metrics, and retain enough raw evidence to audit the conclusion. A passport does not make unlike workloads comparable; it makes the boundary visible.

## What a real benchmark campaign looked like

:::caveat
What a vendor benchmark structurally cannot tell you: your prompt-length distribution, your prefix-sharing rate, your latency service level, your team's operational load, whether the stack implements your model's architecture as well as it implements the reference one it was tuned against, and how any of it ages across the next three releases. Vendor numbers are hypotheses about your workload. The only benchmark that settles anything is yours, on your traffic, on your hardware.
:::

A trustworthy benchmark also needs a complete identity, and the public Qwen3.8-27B campaign shows why.

- **Speed only counted once correctness passed.** The same RTX 5090 target moved from about 71.8 tok/s on an SGLang NONE profile to 151.27 tok/s on a patched TurboQuant 4-bit KV + MTP3 stack. The faster number was accepted only after exact-output, JSON, tool, coding, and multi-turn canaries passed; the stock accelerated launch was a correctness HOLD.
- **Context claims needed the same discipline.** One profile was configured for 262,144 tokens but realized only 42,624 KV tokens. A different long-context profile recovered three needles (facts planted deep in a long input for the model to find) at 261,212 input tokens, with a much longer TTFT.
- **Failed runs were recorded, not hidden.** Two quality runs were discarded because the controller ran out of memory and then the response cache became read-only.

Those are not footnotes. They are part of the result.

The durable posture is to decide with arithmetic and confirm with measurement, in that order. The memory budget tells you what is possible. The workload tells you what is appropriate. Headcount tells you what is sustainable. And a controlled experiment on your own traffic tells you whether you were right, which is a discipline no vendor page can hand you.
