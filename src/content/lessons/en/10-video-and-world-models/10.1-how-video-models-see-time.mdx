[Lesson 4.17](/lessons/4-transformer/4.17-multimodal-vision-encoder/) showed how one still image becomes a few dozen vectors in a decoder's sequence. A video looks like the same problem repeated: a stack of images, so cut each one into patches (small square blocks of pixels) and line them up. That intuition is almost right, and the part where it is wrong is where every interesting decision lives.

Two things make video different. First, a video has a time axis, and the model must be told about it. Second, it has a budget problem that a single image never has: one minute of footage at a camera's native rate is well over a thousand images. Few inputs cost a **vision-language model** (a model that reads images or video together with text and answers in text) more per question than a video, and nearly all of that cost is decided before the first layer of the language model runs.

This lesson's reference system is **Qwen3-VL**, because its technical report describes how it handles video and its released configuration files state the exact shapes. Every number below belongs to Qwen3-VL — the report, or the `Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct` configuration where a specific variant matters — and not to Qwen3.8-27B, the course's running example. Qwen3.8-27B does accept video, through the vision tower that lesson 4.17 traces, but it has no technical report describing how it samples and budgets video, so its video numbers are not used here.

## Sampling: the model never sees every frame

A phone records at 30 frames per second or more. A vision-language model does not read those frames; it reads a **sample** of them, a few frames picked at a fixed rate. The Hugging Face Transformers implementation of the Qwen3-VL video processor samples at **2 frames per second** by default. The report evaluated most of its video benchmarks at 2 fps as well (Charades-STA, a temporal grounding benchmark, at 4 fps). A 60-second clip therefore becomes $\lfloor 60 \times 2 \rfloor = 120$ frames, whatever its native frame rate was.

This is the first place information is thrown away, and nothing reports it. At 2 fps, consecutive samples are half a second apart. An event shorter than that — a ball crossing the frame, a hand flicking a switch — can fall entirely between two samples and never exist for the model. No later layer can recover it. When a video model "misses" something fast, check the sampling rate before blaming the reasoning.

## Tubelets: why two frames become one group

Lesson 4.17 described a Conv3d patch embedding, a convolution whose kernel spans time, height and width at once. Its kernel covers **2 frames × 16 × 16 pixels**, which is exactly what video needs. The released Qwen3-VL-8B configuration states `patch_size` 16 and `temporal_patch_size` 2, so the encoder never tokenizes a single frame on its own. It cuts each pair of consecutive sampled frames into small space-time blocks, often called **tubelets**, each two frames deep and 16 by 16 pixels wide. The 120 sampled frames become **60 temporal groups**.

Two consequences follow:

- **Time moves in one-second steps.** At 2 fps a new group begins once per second, so the effective temporal resolution of what the encoder sees is one step per second, not two.
- **An odd frame count is padded.** An odd number of frames does not split evenly into pairs. The processor repeats the last frame until the count is a multiple of the temporal patch size, so a 2.5-second clip at 2 fps (five frames) becomes three groups, not two and a half.

Pairing frames is not a Qwen invention. The original V-JEPA, which [lesson 10.2](/lessons/10-video-and-world-models/10.2-jepa-predicting-representations/) builds on, tokenized its 16-frame clips with the same 2×16×16 kernel. Pairing halves the sequence. The bet behind it is that two frames half a second apart are usually similar enough for one token to stand for both. That is a design argument, not a measurement, and it is the reason fast motion is the first thing to suffer.

## Patches and the merge: how many tokens per group

Each temporal group is a 2-frame slab of the video. Cut into 16-pixel patches, a 512×384 slab gives a grid of $32 \times 24 = 768$ patches. Those 768 patch vectors go through the vision encoder — the released 8B configuration lists 27 layers at width 1152 — which mixes information between each patch and its neighbours, so every patch vector also describes its surroundings.

Then the **merger** acts. As in Qwen2.5-VL, a two-layer MLP (multilayer perceptron, a small fully connected network, [lesson 2.2](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.2-multilayer-perceptrons-nonlinearity/)) compresses each 2×2 block of neighbouring encoder outputs into one visual token. That token has the language model's width, the vector length every decoder layer expects. The 32×24 grid becomes $16 \times 12 = 192$ tokens per group. Because each group covers two frames, that is an effective 96 tokens per sampled frame.

The two spatial numbers multiply into one rule: every token covers a square of $16 \times 2 = 32$ pixels on each side. So the processor snaps each frame side to a multiple of 32. It rounds to the nearest multiple, and an exact half goes to the even neighbour, as Python's `round` does. For example, a 720-pixel side is 22.5 blocks and becomes 22. A 1280×720 frame therefore yields $40 \times 22 = 880$ tokens per group (for a short clip; a long one is shrunk by the pixel budget below).

Put the three steps together and the whole count is one line. With clip length $d$ seconds, sampling rate $f$, temporal patch $T$, spatial patch $p$, merge $m$ and a frame of $W \times H$ pixels as the encoder receives it:

$$
N = \left\lceil \frac{\lfloor d f \rfloor}{T} \right\rceil \cdot \frac{W}{p m} \cdot \frac{H}{p m}
$$

Read it left to right: the number of temporal groups, times the token grid's width, times its height. For the worked clip, $N = \lceil 120 / 2 \rceil \cdot (512 / 32) \cdot (384 / 32) = 60 \cdot 16 \cdot 12 = 11520$ visual tokens.

:::warning
The tempting mental model is "a video is N images, and each image costs what a photo costs". It overcounts by a factor of the temporal patch size, because a frame never becomes tokens on its own: one token covers two frames and a 32×32-pixel square. It also hides which knob matters most. Frame rate and duration scale the count linearly, at a fixed frame size at the encoder and while the pixel budget is not binding. Frame size scales it quadratically, because the grid grows along both sides. Doubling the resolution of a clip costs as much as quadrupling its frame rate.
:::

## How the model knows when

Space is easy: a token's row and column in the merged grid are its height and width. Time needs more care, and Qwen3-VL uses two mechanisms together.

The first is **interleaved MRoPE**. [Lesson 4.7](/lessons/4-transformer/4.7-rope/) introduced RoPE (rotary position embedding), which encodes position by rotating pairs of query and key dimensions, each pair at its own speed (its frequency). Lesson 4.17 described MRoPE (multimodal RoPE), which gives each element three positions — temporal, height and width — by dedicating different rotary frequencies to each axis.

The Qwen3-VL report says the original arrangement gave each axis one contiguous chunk of dimensions. Each axis then got an imbalanced slice of the frequency spectrum, which the report says hurt long-video understanding. Qwen3-VL **interleaves** the three axes across the dimensions instead, so each axis is represented in both low- and high-frequency bands. The released 8B configuration shows it directly: `mrope_interleaved` is true, with a section split of 24, 20 and 20 rotary pairs.

The second is **text timestamps**. Qwen2.5-VL tied temporal position ids (the integer index that tells the model where an element sits) to absolute time. The report names two problems with that:

- long videos produce very large, sparse position ids;
- the model had to be trained across many frame rates to learn the mapping.

Qwen3-VL instead writes the time as ordinary text in front of each temporal group — a string like `<3.0 seconds>` — and trains with both seconds and hours:minutes:seconds formats. The model reads time the way it reads any other words. The report calls the cost "a modest increase in context length"; the exact number of tokens per timestamp depends on the tokenizer, and the report does not give one.

One more detail does *not* cost sequence length. Qwen3-VL's **DeepStack** takes features from three intermediate levels of the vision encoder. Through their own small mergers, it adds them to the hidden states of the first three language-model layers. The extra visual detail rides the residual stream (the running vector each layer reads from and adds to, [lesson 4.10](/lessons/4-transformer/4.10-residuals-pre-norm-post-norm/)), not the token sequence.

## Budgets: where long video breaks

At a fixed 512×384 frame, 2 fps spends 192 tokens on every second of video. That is linear, and it does not stay small:

<Figure id="video-budget-growth" />

A 30-minute clip at that size and rate is $1800 \cdot 192 = 345600$ tokens. That is past Qwen3-VL's native window of 262,144 tokens before a single word of the question. Real systems defend the budget with a few levers, all visible in the sources.

- **The processor shrinks frames.** The released 8B processor configuration sets a pixel budget of 25,165,824 pixels across all sampled frames. The worked clip uses $120 \cdot 512 \cdot 384 = 23592960$ pixels, so it passes unchanged; a longer clip at the same size is scaled down until it fits. The lab therefore asks for the frame size *as the encoder receives it*.
- **The same budget caps the token count.** Because it counts every pixel of every sampled frame, it allows at most $25165824 / (32^2 \cdot 2) = 12288$ visual tokens per video under the released defaults. The worked clip sits just under that ceiling. Doubling its frame size or frame rate does not raise the count past it, because the processor shrinks frames to fit. The linear growth above is what happens when a deployment raises the budget, as the report's own evaluations did (up to 224K video tokens).
- **The processor caps frames.** The Transformers implementation clamps the sample to between 4 and 768 frames by default.
- **Evaluations set explicit caps.** For its video benchmarks the report capped each video at 2,048 frames and at most 224K video tokens. It also set a "maximum number of tokens per frame" of 768 for VideoMMMU and MMVU and 640 elsewhere — the report's own wording, without saying whether that counts per sampled frame or per two-frame group.

The report also ran a long-video needle-in-a-haystack test, which hides one short moment in a long video and asks the model to find it. On Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct it sampled at 1 fps and adjusted resolution to hold a constant visual token budget. It reports 100% accuracy up to 30 minutes (256K tokens) and 99.5% up to about 2 hours (1M tokens) with YaRN, a method that stretches rotary positions past the trained length.

:::caveat
The pixel budget, the frame clamp and their defaults have changed across processor releases, and the lab models none of them. To know what a real deployment sends, read the `video_grid_thw` the processor returns for your clip rather than trusting arithmetic — including this lesson's.
:::

## Why prefill pays for all of it

Now set a short question beside the worked clip: twenty tokens of question, fifty tokens of answer, 11,520 visual tokens. [Lesson 7.3](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.3-prefill-vs-decode-two-different-machines/) split inference into two phases. **Prefill** processes the whole prompt in parallel; **decode** produces one token at a time. Here the prompt is more than 99% video, so almost every operation in the request happens in prefill. Attention inside prefill grows with the square of that long sequence.

The cache pays too. The KV cache stores one key and one value vector per token, for every layer and every key-value head, so later tokens never recompute them ([lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/) owns the general arithmetic). For the released `Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct` configuration — 36 layers, 8 key-value heads, head dimension 128 — a bf16 cache (2 bytes per number) holds

$$
2 \times 36 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 147456 \ \text{bytes} = 144 \ \text{KiB}
$$

per token. Read the factors as: a key and a value, times layers, times key-value heads, times numbers per head, times bytes per number. So the one-minute clip occupies about 1,620 MiB, roughly 1.6 GiB, before the question is read.

At the same fixed frame size, the 30-minute clip at 1 fps (172,800 tokens) would need about 23.7 GiB. That is a thought experiment, since the real processor's frame clamp and pixel budget would never send it. It still shows why serving video is a memory-planning problem first, and why every lever above exists.
