Lesson 10.2 left a JEPA with an ability that sounds like the start of something bigger: given part of a video, it predicts a representation of the hidden part. A **world model**, in the sense this track uses, is a model that predicts how the world changes — and, if it is to be useful to an agent, how it changes *in response to an action*. V-JEPA 2 asks whether the JEPA recipe, trained mostly on video nobody labelled, can become such a model and drive a real robot arm. This lesson follows that paper and its successor, V-JEPA 2.1, and keeps the measurements apart from the framing.

## Stage one: watch a million hours

The first stage contains no actions at all. V-JEPA 2 pretrains an encoder with the masked-prediction objective from lesson 10.2 on a dataset the authors call VideoMix22M: **over 1 million hours of internet video and 1 million images**, the images duplicated into 16-frame static clips. Encoders range from a ViT-L of about 300 million parameters to a ViT-g of about **1 billion**, with the same predictor for all of them.

The paper reports four scaling ingredients, each measured as average accuracy across its understanding tasks: a larger dataset (2 million to 22 million videos, +1.0 point), a larger model (300 million to 1 billion parameters, +1.5), longer training (90,000 to 252,000 iterations, +0.8), and higher resolution and longer clips, reached cheaply by training at 16 frames and 256×256 and finishing with a short cool-down at 64 frames — 16 seconds — and 384×384. Together they add 4.0 points over the ViT-L baseline, to an 88.2% average across six frozen-probe classification tasks.

What does the encoder know? With an attentive probe on the frozen ViT-g at 384×384 resolution, V-JEPA 2 reaches **77.3% top-1 on Something-Something v2**, a benchmark of motion-defined actions, and **39.7 recall@5 on Epic-Kitchens-100 action anticipation**. Aligned with a language model at the 8-billion-parameter scale, the paper reports 84.0 on PerceptionTest and 76.9 on TempCompass. None of that is planning yet; it is evidence that the representation encodes motion.

## Stage two: learn what actions do

The second stage freezes the encoder and trains a new predictor, **V-JEPA 2-AC** (action-conditioned), on robot data. The data is the **Droid** dataset: teleoperated table-top Franka Panda arms. The paper uses clips of at least four seconds, sampled at 4 fps, which leaves **under 62 hours** of video. It is *unlabelled* in a specific sense: there are no rewards and no success flags, and the 23,000 trajectories include failures.

Each frame becomes a 16×16×1408 feature map from the frozen ViT-g. Each step also carries the arm's **state** (a 7-dimensional vector: position, orientation, gripper) and an **action**, defined as the change in state to the next frame. The predictor is a 24-layer, roughly 300-million-parameter transformer with **block-causal attention**: a token can attend to everything at its own time step and earlier ones, never later.

Training uses two L1 losses on representations. The **teacher-forcing loss** predicts each next frame's features from the true history. The **rollout loss** feeds the predictor its own prediction for one more step, so it learns to survive its own errors — the paper computes it over two steps.

## Planning: search in representation space

Planning needs a goal, and V-JEPA 2-AC takes it as an image. The current frame $x_k$ and goal image $x_g$ are encoded into $z_k$ and $z_g$; with arm state $s_k$ and a candidate action sequence $\hat{a}_{1:T}$, the paper defines a goal-conditioned **energy**:

$$
\mathcal{E}(\hat{a}_{1:T}; z_k, s_k, z_g) = \lVert P(\hat{a}_{1:T}; s_k, z_k) - z_g \rVert_1
$$

Low energy means "this sequence of moves leads somewhere that looks, to the encoder, like the goal". The planner searches for the lowest-energy sequence with the **cross-entropy method** — sample many sequences, keep the best, refit the sampling distribution, repeat — then executes only the **first action**, observes the new frame, and plans again. Each action is limited to a small ball, roughly 13 cm of end-effector motion, because large moves are out of distribution for the model.

The paper also plots the energy for a simple reaching task as a function of one action. The surface is smooth and roughly convex, with its minimum near $(\Delta x, \Delta y) = (0, -0.05)$ when the true action was $(0, -0.1)$: close to right, not exactly right, and smooth enough to search.

## What the robot results show

All tests are zero-shot on Franka arms in two labs that do not appear in Droid, with **10 trials** per task. Reach, grasp and reach-with-object tasks receive one goal image; pick-and-place receives sub-goal images as well.

| Task (average of two labs) | Octo, fine-tuned on Droid | V-JEPA 2-AC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reach | 100% | 100% |
| Grasp: cup / box | 15% / 0% | 65% / 25% |
| Reach with object: cup / box | 15% / 70% | 75% / 75% |
| Pick-and-place: cup / box | 15% / 10% | 80% / 65% |

Against **Cosmos**, an action-conditioned video *generation* model fine-tuned on the same data, the comparison in one lab is starker and explains the design. Planning on one RTX 4090, Cosmos needed **4 minutes per action** with 80 samples per refinement step; V-JEPA 2-AC needed **16 seconds** with 800. A model that must render frames to evaluate a plan pays for every pixel at every step of the search.

:::caveat
Read these numbers at their size. Ten trials give coarse percentages: one trial is ten points. Goals are images someone captured, not instructions. The authors report that the model must infer the action axes from a single uncalibrated camera, and that they **tried camera positions by hand** before settling on one. They also list long-horizon planning as unsolved: prediction errors compound over autoregressive rollouts and the search space grows exponentially with the horizon, which is why pick-and-place needed sub-goal images.
:::

## V-JEPA 2.1: features that know where things are

V-JEPA 2 is strong on *what is happening* and weak on *where exactly*. Its successor's paper measures this on its own ablation baseline — a ViT-L trained with the V-JEPA 2 recipe and evaluated after the first training phase, without a cool-down, so not the released V-JEPA 2 checkpoint. With linear probes it scores **22.2 mIoU** for semantic segmentation on ADE20K and **0.682 RMSE** for depth on NYUv2, and its feature maps look noisy and fragmented.

The diagnosis is about where the loss lives. In V-JEPA 2 the predictor outputs a vector for every token, visible and masked, but the loss uses **only the masked ones**. The authors' hypothesis: visible tokens therefore have no reason to describe their own patch, and can serve as global aggregators instead — the role register tokens play in some image transformers. They test it by adding a **context loss** on visible tokens, weighted by distance to the nearest masked token $d_{\min}$:

$$
\lambda_i = \frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{d_{\min}(i, M)}}
$$

so visible tokens near a hidden region are supervised most. With that loss alone, segmentation rose to 33.8 mIoU and depth error fell to 0.474 — and Something-Something v2 accuracy fell from 72.8% to 62.5%. Dense features were bought with global understanding. The second ingredient, **deep self-supervision**, applies both losses at four levels of the encoder (three intermediate blocks and the output), and restored SSv2 to 72.1% while improving segmentation further.

<Figure id="dense-recipe-ablation" />

The other ingredients are practical. A **multi-modal tokenizer** gives images their own 2D 16×16 patch embedding and videos a 3D 16×16×2 one, plus a learned modality token, where V-JEPA 2 had duplicated each image into a 16-frame static video. The image data grows from 1 million to 142 million images (the VisionMix-163M mix), and the largest encoder is a 2-billion-parameter ViT-G, with smaller ViT-B and ViT-L models distilled from it. The full ViT-G reports **0.307 RMSE** on NYUv2 depth with a linear probe, **77.7%** on Something-Something v2, 7.71 mAP on Ego4D short-term object-interaction anticipation and 40.8 recall@5 on Epic-Kitchens-100. That last number needs the larger model: at the same 1-billion-parameter size, the paper's own table gives V-JEPA 2.1 38.4 against V-JEPA 2's 39.7.

The robot test reuses V-JEPA 2-AC's code and task files with the new encoder, grasping a cup in one lab, averaged over 10 tasks. With the same planner settings, success rose from **60% to 70%**. With an 8-step planning horizon and fewer samples it reached **80%**, while V-JEPA 2 got worse at longer horizons — the source of the paper's "+20%" headline. Both statements are in the paper; only the first compares like with like.

## Shown, and claimed

Shown: a representation learned from unlabelled internet video, plus under 62 hours of unlabelled robot video, supports zero-shot planning of short manipulation tasks from image goals. Its success matched or beat a fine-tuned Octo policy on every task in two labs, and matched or beat the Cosmos video generator on every task in one lab while planning each action in 16 seconds instead of 4 minutes. Claimed, by framing: that this is a path toward agents that understand and plan in the physical world. The gap between the two is long-horizon planning, language goals and scale of evidence — all named by the authors themselves. Lesson 10.4 turns to one of them: what happens when the target of prediction is not a video frame but the meaning of a sentence.
