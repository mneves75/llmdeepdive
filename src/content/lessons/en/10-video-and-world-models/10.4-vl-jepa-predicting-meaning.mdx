Every vision-language model in this course so far answers the way a language model does. Lesson 10.1's Qwen3-VL turns a video into visual tokens, places them in a sequence with the question, and then generates the answer one token at a time; it was trained, as lesson 5.1 described, with cross-entropy on the next token. The JEPAs of lessons 10.2 and 10.3 never predict tokens, so on their own they cannot answer in words. **VL-JEPA**, published at ICLR 2026, puts the two ideas together: a vision-language model whose training target is the *embedding* of the answer, not its words.

## The four parts

The paper names the parts after JEPA's own vocabulary, and instantiates them from existing models.

- **X-encoder** — maps the visual input to embeddings. A frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L of 304 million parameters, with frames at 256×256.
- **Predictor** — reads the visual embeddings together with the text query and predicts the answer's embedding. It is initialised from the last 8 transformer layers of Llama-3.2-1B, giving 490 million trainable parameters, with the **causal mask disabled** so that vision and query tokens attend to each other in both directions. Its outputs are average-pooled into one vector.
- **Y-encoder** — embeds the real answer text as the prediction target, initialised from EmbeddingGemma-300M and trained with a learning-rate multiplier of 0.05.
- **Y-decoder** — turns a predicted embedding back into text. It takes no part in the main training and runs at inference only when a person needs words.

The whole system has 1.6 billion parameters. The change from a classical model is one line. A token-generative model minimises a distance in data space, VL-JEPA in embedding space:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VLM}} = D(\hat{Y}, Y) \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{VL-JEPA}} = D(\hat{S}_Y, S_Y)
$$

## A different defence against collapse

Lesson 10.2's collapse problem returns, because the Y-encoder is trained too: if it mapped every answer to the same point, the predictor could match it perfectly. VL-JEPA does *not* use the moving-average target of the V-JEPA papers. It trains predictor and Y-encoder jointly with a **bidirectional InfoNCE** loss, the contrastive loss CLIP made standard. InfoNCE splits into an alignment term, which pulls each prediction toward its own target, and a uniformity term, which pushes the embeddings in a batch apart — and that second term is what rules out collapse. The paper's ablation found InfoNCE generally better than cosine, L1 or L2 losses (cosine won on VQA), and notes that only InfoNCE carries its own anti-collapse term, so only it works with an unfrozen Y-encoder.

## Why predict an embedding

The paper's argument is about the shape of the target. Ask "What will happen if I flip this light switch down?" and both "the lamp is turned off" and "room will go dark" are right. As token sequences they share no tokens; a cross-entropy loss treats them as two separate targets the model must spread probability over. A good text encoder puts them close together, so the predictor needs to hit one region of embedding space instead of several disjoint ones. That is an argument about learning difficulty, and the next section is the experiment that tests it.

## The controlled comparison

The paper trains two models with **everything held equal except the objective**: the same frozen Perception Encoder (a ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels, 16 frames per video, no tiling), the same data mixture, batch size 128, the same iterations and learning-rate schedule. VL-JEPA uses a 0.5-billion-parameter predictor initialised from layers 8 to 16 of Llama-3.2-1B. The baseline follows the PerceptionLM recipe: next-token prediction with cross-entropy using the full Llama-3.2-1B. Captioning is measured by CIDEr averaged over YouCook2, MSR-VTT and PVD-Bench; classification by top-5 accuracy averaged over three benchmarks.

| Samples seen | Model | Captioning (CIDEr) | Classification (top-5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 thousand | VL-JEPA | 1.23 | 14.9% |
| 500 thousand | Token-generative VLM | 1.35 | 14.0% |
| 15 million | VL-JEPA | 14.8 | 41.0% |
| 15 million | Token-generative VLM | 7.1 | 27.2% |

At the start the two are level, with the baseline slightly ahead on captioning. By 5 million samples VL-JEPA has reached 14.7 CIDEr and 35.3%, and the gap then holds. This is the strongest evidence in the paper, because it isolates the variable. It is also small: a 1-billion-parameter language model, zero-shot captioning and classification, one data mixture. The paper describes VL-JEPA as using roughly half the trainable parameters of the baseline.

## Selective decoding

Because VL-JEPA predicts an answer embedding in a single forward pass, a video stream yields a continuous **stream of meanings**, available at every step without generating a word. The paper proposes smoothing that stream and decoding only when it shifts. Its experiment tests the idea offline: it clusters each video's embedding sequence into segments of similar meaning and decodes once per segment, at its midpoint. Average pooling of the embeddings helped both this and uniform decoding. Tested on 218 EgoExo4D videos of procedural activity, averaging 6 minutes and about 143 annotated actions each, selective decoding at about 0.35 Hz — one decode every 2.85 seconds — matched the quality of uniform decoding at 1 Hz: about **2.85 times fewer decoding operations**. A token-generative model has no cheap way to know whether its answer has changed short of generating it.

## One space, several tasks

The same embedding space serves tasks that classical VLMs and CLIP-style models split between them. **Retrieval and classification** work by comparing a video's predicted embedding with embeddings of candidate texts. After its first, caption-only training stage, the model the paper calls VL-JEPA-BASE beats CLIP, SigLIP2 and Perception Encoder on average zero-shot accuracy over 8 video classification datasets and average recall@1 over 8 text-to-video retrieval datasets. After supervised fine-tuning, VL-JEPA-SFT answers questions **discriminatively**: it encodes each candidate answer with the Y-encoder and picks the one nearest its prediction. On GQA, TallyQA, POPE and POPEv2 it is comparable to InstructBLIP and Qwen-VL. Note the format: choosing among given answers is not the same task as writing one freely.

## Closing the track: two ways to predict

This track began with a vision-language model that turns a minute of video into 11,520 tokens and generates its answer word by word. It ends with models that never generate during training at all. It is tempting to frame them as rivals. The evidence is narrower than that, in both directions.

| | Autoregressive language and vision-language models | Embedding-prediction models (JEPA family) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Training target | The next token, in a finite vocabulary | A representation of the hidden or future part |
| What comes out | Text, directly | Vectors; text or actions need a decoder or a planner |
| Collapse | Not a risk: the targets are fixed tokens | A central risk, handled by EMA and stop-gradient or by contrastive terms |
| Measured strengths in this track | Long-video needle-in-a-haystack retrieval up to 256K tokens, on Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B (10.1) | Frozen-probe motion understanding; zero-shot short-horizon robot planning, 16 seconds per action against a video generator's 4 minutes; more sample-efficient captioning in one controlled small-scale study; fewer decodes when streaming |
| Main limitation seen in this track | Cost grows with every token: one minute of video is 11,520 tokens of prefill and cache (10.1) | No direct text or action output; reasoning, tool use and agentic behaviours still need broader evaluation — the VL-JEPA authors' own list |

:::insight
The two families are less separate than the framing suggests. V-JEPA 2 is aligned with a language model to answer video questions; VL-JEPA's predictor is made of Llama layers; Qwen3-VL's vision encoder starts from SigLIP-2, which the V-JEPA 2 paper classes with image–text contrastive models, not pixel generators. What actually differs is where the loss is computed — on tokens, or on representations — and each choice buys something specific.
:::

Hold every claim in this track to the same test you would hold a benchmark to: what was measured, in which setting, against what. Prediction in representation space has earned its place with specific, reproducible results. Whether it becomes the route to systems that understand and act in the physical world is the authors' argument, made in good faith and not yet settled by evidence.
