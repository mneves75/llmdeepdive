You ship a support assistant in two languages. Same product, same system prompt, same model, translated faithfully. A month later the finance report shows the Portuguese deployment costing noticeably more per conversation than the English one. Nobody wrote different code. The difference was decided years earlier, by whichever corpus the tokenizer's merges were counted over. This lesson explains why, and shows how to measure and price the difference yourself.

## Why does the same sentence cost different amounts?

The question *how many tokens is this text* has no answer until a tokenizer is named. A token is not a stable linguistic unit. It is an entry in one model's fixed vocabulary, produced under one normalization policy and one ordered merge list, as [lesson 1.3](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.3-bpe-step-by-step/) established. Two models can display identical decoded text and occupy different numbers of positions doing it.

Work the example concretely, with the measurement step made explicit, because it is the step people skip. Take an English sentence and its Portuguese translation, run both through the tokenizer that ships with Qwen3.8-27B, and record the counts. Suppose your measurement returns 18 tokens for the English and 26 for the Portuguese. That is an illustrative pair of numbers, not a published fact, and exactly the kind of thing you must measure rather than quote.

The ratio is about 1.44: in this example the Portuguese text consumes forty-four percent more billable input tokens and context positions. It does not prove exactly forty-four percent more prefill compute or latency. (Prefill is the model's first pass over the whole prompt, before it writes anything.) Those depend on architecture, sequence length, batching, kernels, and hardware, so measure the deployed runtime.

## Turning a token count into money

Now convert positions into money. As of August 2026, Cloudflare Workers AI listed Qwen3.8-27B at USD 0.45 per million input tokens and USD 3.20 per million output tokens. One million requests at 18 input tokens each is 18 million tokens:

$$
18 \times 0.45 = 8.10 \quad \text{(USD, input, one million requests)}
$$

$$
26 \times 0.45 = 11.70 \quad \text{(USD, input, one million requests)}
$$

A difference of USD 3.60 per million requests. Per request it is invisible, three ten-thousandths of a cent, and that is exactly why it never gets caught in review. At volume it is a structural tax levied on one of your two user populations. It grows with every system prompt, every retrieved document, and every conversation turn that gets replayed into the context.

:::caveat
Both the counts and the arithmetic above illustrate the method; they are not measurements you may cite. The rate is date-stamped for a reason: published prices change, and a figure quoted without its date is a liability. What lasts is the procedure: measure with the deployed tokenizer, multiply by the current published rate, and state when you looked.
:::

## Why do rules of thumb fail?

Here is the shortcut nearly everyone takes. Somebody estimates tokens as characters divided by four, or words divided by 0.75. Those ratios were calibrated on English prose and they collapse elsewhere:

- Portuguese inflection and accented characters segment differently.
- Chinese and Japanese have no spaces to anchor pieces.
- Source code, long numbers, URLs, tables, and emoji all fragment in ways no prose-derived constant anticipates.

Worse, the rule fails *unevenly*. It under-counts precisely the languages that are already paying more, which turns a budgeting error into a fairness problem. The correction is not a better constant. It is to stop estimating: the tokenizer is a few lines of code away and returns the true count in milliseconds.

## How do tokenizer families differ?

Tokenizer families reach their counts in different ways. Many pipelines first split on whitespace or punctuation and then learn subwords inside the pieces. That bakes language-specific assumptions into preprocessing without anyone deciding it.

**SentencePiece** was designed to train directly from raw sentences. It represents whitespace as an explicit symbol, so tokenization and detokenization form a closed, reversible process. It is a toolkit rather than a single algorithm. It supports BPE-style merges, and it also supports the **Unigram** model. Unigram starts from a large candidate vocabulary, gives each piece a probability, and repeatedly removes the pieces whose loss hurts the corpus likelihood least, until the vocabulary shrinks to its target size. Unigram allows several segmentations of a string and can sample among them, which BPE's ordered merges do not.

**Byte-level** approaches, the family Qwen3.8-27B belongs to, solve coverage from below. Qwen's ByteLevel pre-tokenizer provides byte-derived units for every UTF-8 byte of valid Unicode text after NFC normalization, so unseen scripts need not fall back to an unknown symbol. Its public tokenizer is still a text interface, not an arbitrary-binary decoder, and the published BPE model sets byte fallback to false.

Coverage does not imply efficiency. A familiar English fragment may be one token, while an uncommon grapheme (one character as a reader sees it, which may be several code points) expands into several byte pieces. That unevenness is the mechanism behind the invoice above.

## What else changes the count?

Normalization moves counts before any of this happens. Canonically equivalent Unicode strings can differ in code points, and Qwen's NFC normalizer collapses the two *café* spellings examined in [lesson 1.1](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.1-why-computers-cant-read/) before BPE. Lowercasing, compatibility normalization (which also merges look-alikes such as a ligature and its letters), whitespace cleanup, and accent handling each erase distinctions that some task somewhere needs.

Then special tokens add a layer that is entirely invisible in the text box. Beginning and end markers, role separators, tool-call delimiters, and chat-template scaffolding all occupy positions, and all are billed. For a model with thinking and non-thinking modes, the template itself differs between them, which [lesson 6.2](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.2-supervised-fine-tuning-and-instruction-data/) develops.

:::warning
Measuring the text you typed gives the wrong number. The billable, context-consuming object is the fully assembled request that goes on the wire: system prompt, chat template markers, tool schemas, retrieved context, conversation history, and only then your sentence. Teams routinely audit the last of those and are then surprised by the total. Serialize the actual payload and tokenize that.
:::

## How do you compare tokenizers fairly?

When comparing tokenizers seriously, use representative corpora per language and domain. Report distributions rather than an anecdote: median and tail sequence lengths, the share of byte-derived fragments, and how badly your important domain terms fragment. Check that decoding respects the tokenizer's declared normalization contract, and that special tokens cannot be forged from user text.

The conclusion is firm and slightly uncomfortable. Tokens are model-specific packaging. SentencePiece, BPE, Unigram, and byte-level tokenization are engineering choices that trade vocabulary size against coverage, sequence length, and inductive bias (the assumptions a design builds in before seeing data). None of them delivers a language-neutral natural unit. The consequences land unevenly on real users, and the only defensible practice is to measure the exact request against the exact deployed tokenizer, then price it at a rate you can date.
