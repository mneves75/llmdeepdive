Around 2013 a demonstration circulated that convinced a great many people that machines had started to understand language. Take the vector for *king*, subtract the vector for *man*, add the vector for *woman*, and search the vocabulary for whatever lies nearest the result. Out comes *queen*. It is a striking result, and this lesson takes it apart carefully, because the usual retelling omits the one detail that decides how impressive it is.

## Where did those word vectors come from?

First the machinery that produced those vectors. Before contextual models (models whose vector for a word changes with the sentence around it), **static word embeddings** gave each word one fixed vector. They made a concrete claim: the patterns of which words occur near which others can become geometry. Instead of treating each word as an unrelated symbol, these methods learned a dense vector per word from how words occur around one another. The resulting neighbourhoods supported similarity search, classification, and reuse in other systems.

**word2vec** names a family of predictive training objectives and the tricks that made them fast. It has two variants:

- In **continuous bag-of-words** (CBOW), the surrounding context words predict the centre word.
- In **skip-gram**, the centre word predicts the nearby context words.

Computing a full softmax (the function that turns scores into probabilities) over the whole vocabulary for every training pair would be ruinously slow. Practical implementations approximate it. **Negative sampling** trains the model to tell the real target word (the context word in skip-gram, the centre word in CBOW) apart from a few randomly drawn "negative" words. **Hierarchical softmax** arranges the vocabulary in a tree, so each prediction costs a few yes-or-no decisions.

One detail matters later in this lesson: the method maintains **two** embedding tables, an input one and an output one, with different roles. Which table you export, or whether you average them, changes your results.

## What do the training settings change?

The context window, how many neighbouring words count as context, defines what the geometry captures. A narrow window emphasizes syntactic and functional similarity; a wide one drifts toward topical association. Subsampling frequent words and the negative-sampling distribution both change which distinctions receive gradient (the training signal). Corpus domain moves everything: legal, medical, news, and conversational text produce different neighbourhoods for the same word. There is no such thing as *the* word2vec embedding without the training configuration attached.

## How does GloVe learn from counts instead?

**GloVe** starts from the other end, with a global word-context co-occurrence matrix: a table counting how often each word appears near each other word across the whole corpus. Its central observation is that *ratios* of co-occurrence probabilities carry the informative signal. Raw counts are dominated by frequency. Asking whether two probe words relate differently to a shared context word says more.

The GloVe paper's own example uses the probe words *ice* and *steam*. Take the context word *solid*: it appears near *ice* far more often than near *steam*, so the ratio of the two probabilities, P(solid | ice) divided by P(solid | steam), is large. For *gas* the ratio is small. For *water*, which appears near both, and for *fashion*, which appears near neither, the ratio is close to 1. Only the context words that tell ice from steam move the ratio away from 1, and that is the signal GloVe trains on.

Training fits vectors whose dot products, plus bias terms, approximate a function of the logged counts. GloVe bridges two traditions: factoring a big matrix of counts, and learning from local context. In practice the two families produce broadly comparable geometry.

## Re-running the analogy as an experiment

Now return to the demonstration, and run it as an experiment rather than a story. You compute the point $v_{king} - v_{man} + v_{woman}$ and search for its nearest neighbour by cosine similarity (which compares the directions of two vectors).

Here is the omitted detail: the standard evaluation protocol **excludes the three query words** from the candidate set. That rule is not cosmetic. Linzen's 2016 re-evaluation removed the exclusion and found drastic accuracy drops, including zero accuracy for several categories.

Why can the exclusion matter so much? Subtracting *man* and adding *woman* may move the point only modestly relative to the surrounding vocabulary, so an input word can remain highly competitive. Excluding it can therefore change the winner. The important claim is the measured protocol effect, not that one particular query word must always be nearest.

## What does the analogy actually show?

This does not make the finding fake. A consistent offset across many pairs is a real and useful geometric property. That one linear operation can transfer a relation across pairs is surprising, given that nobody supervised it. What it does mean is that the demonstration measures a *tendency* under a specific search protocol, not the execution of a symbolic rule.

The corrected claim reads: under cosine similarity, with query words excluded, the analogy target is frequently the nearest neighbour for certain relation types in certain corpora. Longer, duller, and defensible in the sense [lesson 0.8](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.8-how-to-read-ml-paper/) demanded.

The sensitivity does not stop there:

- Results shift with vector normalization, with the choice of nearest-neighbour rule, with vocabulary frequency thresholds, and with which relation types the benchmark happens to contain.
- Some relations form clusters rather than a single reusable direction.
- Morphology (word structure, like plural endings) and raw frequency explain part of the performance.
- A miss in first place may still place a sensible answer second.

Hand-picked examples therefore systematically overstate reliability.

:::warning
Vector arithmetic does not reveal a neutral fact about language. It reveals a fact about a corpus, and corpora carry their history. Occupational and identity associations learned from text reproduce stereotypes just as faithfully as they reproduce capital-city relations, and expressing an association in linear algebra does not make it neutral. Removing one measurable direction does not remove the information, which is spread across many dimensions and combined nonlinearly. Suppressing an association can also destroy distinctions a task legitimately needs. Where the data came from, and how the system behaves downstream, matter far more than a satisfying two-dimensional projection.
:::

## What survives in a modern model?

:::note
Qwen3.8-27B has no word2vec stage anywhere in it. Its 248,320-row embedding tensor is optimized end to end with the model, with no separate pre-training of word vectors. The tokenizer maps 248,077 ids, and the published artifacts do not reveal what signal the 243 unmapped rows received. What survives from this era is not the algorithm but the finding: distributional evidence, given a continuous space and an objective, arranges itself into usable structure. And one structural detail survives literally. word2vec's split between an input and an output table is the same asymmetry as this model's untied embedding and language-model head from [lesson 1.5](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.5-embeddings-meaning-as-geometry/), at a far larger scale and learned end to end rather than on its own.
:::

Modern transformers use contextual states rather than one fixed vector per word, but the discipline of interpreting them carries over unchanged. Linear probes (small linear classifiers trained on a model's internal vectors) and vector operations can expose real regularities. What they mean depends on the layer, the metric, the corpus, and the task.

For a reproducible comparison, publish the corpus snapshot, the window definition, the vocabulary threshold, the negative-sampling policy, which table was exported, the similarity metric, and the exact analogy-scoring rule, including whether query words were excluded. Re-running one famous equation without that contract is a demonstration, and it should be labelled as one.
