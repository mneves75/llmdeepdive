Two teams evaluate language models on the same corpus. One reports a perplexity of 7.4, the other 4.2, and the second declares victory. Before accepting that, ask whether the two numbers average surprise over the same kind of event. A tokenizer is part of a model's learned interface: changing it changes the token sequence, the embedding indices, and the probabilities being scored. Per-token perplexities from different model-tokenizer pairs therefore need not be directly rankable. This lesson builds perplexity from scratch so you can see exactly where the comparison breaks.

## How do you measure surprise?

Information theory turns probability into a measure of surprise. An event of probability $p$ carries self-information $-\log p$. Likely events are unsurprising; unlikely ones carry more information when they occur.

A toy case makes the scale concrete. A fair coin landing heads has probability one half, and in base-two logarithms its surprise is exactly 1 bit. An outcome with probability one eighth carries 3 bits, because you would need three fair coin flips to single it out. An outcome the model was certain of carries no surprise at all.

The base of the logarithm chooses the unit: base two gives **bits**, the natural logarithm gives **nats**. Deep-learning libraries almost always use nats, and papers frequently quote bits. The conversion factor $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ is responsible for a large share of the wrong numbers in circulation.

## Entropy and cross-entropy

The **entropy** of a discrete distribution is its expected surprise: each outcome's surprise, weighted by how likely that outcome is.

$$
H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x).
$$

A distribution concentrated on one outcome has zero entropy. A uniform distribution over many outcomes has the highest entropy available at that size. For a categorical distribution over $V$ candidates, the uniform baseline has perplexity $V$ and surprise $\log_2 V$ bits per event. Apply that anchor only after stating which candidates the runtime includes; Qwen's tokenizer maps 248,077 ids while its output tensor has 248,320 rows.

To score a model's distribution $q$ against outcomes actually drawn from $p$, use **cross-entropy**:

$$
H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x).
$$

On a real corpus, the observed next token stands in for the data distribution. So cross-entropy reduces to averaging the negative log probability the model gave to each token that actually appeared. That is the mean negative log-likelihood, the ordinary training loss (the number training tries to push down). Assign tiny probability to what happened and pay heavily; assign high probability and pay little.

Cross-entropy splits into two parts: $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p \Vert q)$. The first term is the irreducible uncertainty in the data. The second, the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, is the extra cost of using the wrong distribution. No model can drive the first term to zero, which is why a perplexity of one on natural text would be evidence of leakage rather than genius.

## From cross-entropy to perplexity

**Perplexity** exponentiates the average. In nats, $\operatorname{PPL} = \exp(H)$; in bits, $\operatorname{PPL} = 2^{H}$. If the model faced $k$ equally likely options at every step, perplexity would be $k$, which is where the phrase *effective branching factor* comes from. Real distributions are not uniform, so treat it as an intuition rather than a count of candidates.

## Why does a different tokenizer break the comparison?

Now the two teams. For pair A, let the total negative log-likelihood be $S_A=2886$ bits over $N_A=1000$ tokens:

$$
\frac{S_A}{N_A}=\frac{2886}{1000}=2.886\ \text{bits per token},\qquad \operatorname{PPL}_A=2^{2.886}\approx 7.4.
$$

Pair B may produce $N_B=1400$ tokens over the same displayed text, but it has its own total surprise $S_B$:

$$
\operatorname{PPL}_B=2^{S_B/N_B}=2^{S_B/1400}.
$$

You cannot substitute $S_A=2886$ into the second formula. A different tokenizer defines different prediction events, and arbitrary ids no longer address the rows learned for them. Holding 2886 fixed would be a useful arithmetic demonstration of how much the denominator matters, yielding about 4.2. It would not be a valid model evaluation across tokenizers. Real comparisons measure both numerators and use a common unit.

:::insight
This is why per-token perplexity is not even comparable across *languages* for a single model. Measure Qwen3.8-27B on an English document and a faithful Portuguese translation, and the Portuguese may occupy more tokens. The counts in [lesson 1.4](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.4-sentencepiece-byte-level-token-counts/) were illustrative, so measure rather than assume. The two perplexities still describe different token events. For the same evaluation byte corpus across model-tokenizer pairs, **bits per byte** (BPB) uses a common denominator: $\operatorname{BPB}_A=S_A/B$ and $\operatorname{BPB}_B=S_B/B$. This makes the unit comparable across segmentations. It does not make the models' probability assignments identical, nor does it make translations byte-for-byte equivalent.
:::

The classic wrong fix deserves naming because it is subtle. Faced with the mismatch, people often compare per-*word* perplexity instead, reasoning that words are language-neutral. They are not, as [lesson 1.2](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.2-tokenization-i/) showed. Word boundaries are a convention that several major languages do not use, and inflected languages pack more into one word than analytic ones. Bytes are not perfect either: a script whose characters cost three UTF-8 bytes is charged more per character than one whose characters cost one. But bytes are at least defined identically for every model and every language, and the definition is checkable.

## What else makes two perplexities incomparable?

Comparability has further boundaries beyond tokenization. Perplexity depends on:

- the evaluation corpus;
- how beginning and end tokens are handled;
- the context length;
- whether a sliding window recomputes overlapping regions;
- which positions are masked out of the loss.

Two published numbers that differ in any of those are not rankable. The honest response to a mismatch is to report the discrepancy rather than to average it away.

Leakage is the other hidden destroyer. Evaluation text duplicated in the training data measures memory alongside generalization, and at modern corpus scales, assuming a clean split is optimistic. Domain mismatch cuts both ways. A strong model can look weak on text unlike anything it will serve, or flatteringly strong on text that resembles its training distribution.

:::warning
A common mistake is to think lower perplexity means a better assistant. Perplexity answers one narrow, well-posed question: how surprised was this model by these tokens, under this protocol? It says nothing about instruction following, factual reliability, refusal behavior, tool use, or cost to serve. Post-training in track 6 routinely improves assistant quality while making base-language metrics move in confusing directions. Keep the narrow question narrow, and evaluate the broad claims with evidence that actually addresses them.
:::

Report perplexity the way [lesson 1.6](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.6-word2vec-glove-analogy/) asked you to report an analogy result: with the corpus provenance, the token count, the model and tokenizer versions, the context policy, and enough precision to support the conclusion without exaggerating it. A number without its protocol is not a measurement.
