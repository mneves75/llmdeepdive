Two teams evaluate language models on the same corpus. One reports a perplexity of 7.4, the other reports 4.2. The second team declares victory. Before accepting that, ask a question nobody asked in the meeting: were the two numbers counting the same events? This lesson builds the measurement carefully enough to answer that, and the answer turns out to be that the second team may have changed nothing but its tokenizer.

Information theory turns probability into a measure of surprise. An event of probability $p$ carries self-information $-\log p$: likely events are unsurprising, unlikely ones carry more information when they occur. The base of the logarithm chooses the unit — base two gives **bits**, the natural logarithm gives **nats**. Deep-learning libraries almost always use nats, papers frequently quote bits, and the conversion factor $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ is responsible for a healthy fraction of the wrong numbers in circulation.

The **entropy** of a discrete distribution is expected surprise:

$$
H(p)=-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x).
$$

A distribution concentrated on one outcome has zero entropy. A uniform distribution over many outcomes has the highest entropy available at that size. That upper bound is a useful anchor for our specimen: a model that had learned nothing at all, spreading probability uniformly across Qwen3.8-27B's 248,320 vocabulary entries, would achieve a perplexity of exactly 248,320, which is about 17.9 bits of surprise per token. Every bit below that is learning.

To score a model distribution $q$ against outcomes drawn from $p$, use **cross-entropy**:

$$
H(p,q)=-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x).
$$

On a real corpus the observed next token stands in for the data distribution, so this reduces to averaging the negative log probability the model gave to each token that actually appeared. That is mean negative log-likelihood, the ordinary training loss. Assign tiny probability to what happened and pay heavily; assign high probability and pay little. Cross-entropy decomposes as $H(p,q) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p \Vert q)$: irreducible uncertainty in the data, plus the extra cost of using the wrong distribution. No model can drive the first term to zero, which is why a perplexity of one on natural text would be evidence of leakage rather than genius.

**Perplexity** exponentiates the average. In nats, $\operatorname{PPL} = \exp(H)$; in bits, $\operatorname{PPL} = 2^{H}$. If the model faced $k$ equally likely options at every step, perplexity would be $k$, which is where the phrase *effective branching factor* comes from. Real distributions are not uniform, so treat it as an intuition rather than a count of candidates.

Now work the opening puzzle all the way through. Suppose a model assigns a document a total surprise of 2886 bits. That total is a property of the model and the byte string, and it does not care how anyone chops the text up. Tokenizer A produces 1000 tokens:

$$
\frac{2886}{1000}=2.886\ \text{bits per token},\qquad 2^{2.886}\approx 7.4.
$$

Tokenizer B produces 1400 tokens for the identical document:

$$
\frac{2886}{1400}=2.061\ \text{bits per token},\qquad 2^{2.061}\approx 4.2.
$$

Same model, same text, same total uncertainty, and a perplexity that fell by more than forty percent. Nothing improved. The denominator changed. A tokenizer that fragments more finely spreads the same surprise over more, individually easier, predictions — and lesson 1.4 already showed that fragmentation varies enormously by language and domain.

:::insight
This is why per-token perplexity is not even comparable across *languages* for a single model. Measure Qwen3.8-27B on an English document and on its faithful Portuguese translation, and the Portuguese text will occupy more tokens for the same content, dividing its total surprise across a longer denominator. The two numbers describe different corridors of doors. The repair is to normalize by something tokenizer-independent: **bits per byte**, computed as total surprise in bits divided by the number of UTF-8 bytes in the document. That quantity survives a change of segmentation, which is exactly why serious cross-model comparisons report it.
:::

The classic wrong turn here deserves naming because it is subtle. Faced with the mismatch, people often try to fix it by comparing per-*word* perplexity instead, reasoning that words are language-neutral. They are not, as lesson 1.2 showed: word boundaries are a convention that several major languages do not use, and inflected languages pack more into one word than analytic ones. Bytes are not perfect either — a script whose characters cost three UTF-8 bytes is charged more per character than one whose characters cost one — but bytes are at least defined identically for every model and every language, and the definition is checkable.

Comparability has further boundaries beyond tokenization. Perplexity depends on the evaluation corpus, on how beginning and end tokens are handled, on the context length, on whether a sliding window recomputes overlapping regions, and on which positions are masked out of the loss. Two published numbers that differ in any of those are not rankable, and the honest response to a mismatch is to report the discrepancy rather than to average it away.

Leakage is the other quiet destroyer. Evaluation text duplicated in training measures memory alongside generalization, and at modern corpus scales assuming a clean split is optimistic. Domain mismatch cuts both ways: a strong model can look weak on text unlike anything it will serve, or flatteringly strong on text that resembles its training distribution.

:::warning
The misconception worth naming is that lower perplexity means a better assistant. Perplexity answers one narrow, well-posed question: how surprised was this model by these tokens, under this protocol? It says nothing about instruction following, factual reliability, refusal behavior, tool use, or cost to serve. Post-training in track 6 routinely improves assistant quality while making base-language metrics move in confusing directions. Keep the narrow question narrow, and evaluate the broad claims with evidence that actually addresses them.
:::

Report perplexity the way lesson 1.6 asked you to report an analogy result: with the corpus provenance, the token count, the model and tokenizer versions, the context policy, and enough precision to support the conclusion without exaggerating it. A number without its protocol is not a measurement.
