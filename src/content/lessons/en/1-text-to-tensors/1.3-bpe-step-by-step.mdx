[Lesson 1.2](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.2-tokenization-i/) said that subword pieces are learned from a corpus (a large collection of text) and left the learning unexplained. This lesson does it by hand, with a corpus small enough to count on paper. The algorithm is BPE (byte-pair encoding), and it is simple; the interesting part is what happens *between* its merges, where the mistakes usually start.

## Counting pairs in a tiny corpus

Take a corpus containing three words with these frequencies: *low* five times, *lower* twice, and *lowest* three times. Split every word into characters and append an end-of-word marker, written here as an underscore. The marker lets a piece that ends a word be told apart from the same piece in the middle of one. The corpus is now `l o w _` at count 5, `l o w e r _` at count 2, and `l o w e s t _` at count 3.

Count every adjacent pair, weighting by word frequency. The pair `l o` appears in all three words, giving $5 + 2 + 3 = 10$. The pair `o w` likewise gives 10. The pair `w _` appears only in *low*, giving 5. The pair `w e` appears in *lower* and *lowest*, giving 5. Then `e r` gives 2, `r _` gives 2, `e s` gives 3, `s t` gives 3, and `t _` gives 3. Two pairs are tied at the top, so the implementation's tie-breaking rule decides; take `l o`.

## Why the counts must be redone after every merge

Merge the winning pair everywhere. The corpus becomes `lo w _` at 5, `lo w e r _` at 2, and `lo w e s t _` at 3.

Here is the step people skip: **the counts must be recomputed**. The pair `l o` no longer exists anywhere, and a new unit `lo` has appeared with new neighbours. Recounting gives `lo w` at 10, which now wins outright. Merge it, and the corpus becomes `low _` at 5, `low e r _` at 2, `low e s t _` at 3. The vocabulary has gained the piece `low`.

Recount again. Now `low _` occurs 5 times and `low e` occurs $2 + 3 = 5$ times, tied. Below them, `e s`, `s t`, and `t _` have 3 apiece, and `e r` and `r _` have 2. Suppose the tie-break takes `low _`, producing a single unit for the standalone word *low*. The fourth merge then takes `low e`, giving the shared stem of *lower* and *lowest*.

Four merges in, the algorithm has found something that looks like a **morpheme**, the smallest unit of a word that carries meaning (like *un-* or *-est*). It found it for a purely statistical reason: the two halves of that string kept appearing side by side.

Every decision above is **greedy**: the algorithm picks the best pair under the current split and never reconsiders. It never plans toward a globally optimal vocabulary. Tie-breaking, the representation of word boundaries, the normalization applied beforehand, and the choice of base units are all implementation decisions. That is why two tools that both say BPE need not produce identical tokenizers.

## Training learns merges; encoding replays them

Now the distinction that matters in production. **Training** learns a vocabulary *and an ordered list of merge rules*. **Encoding**, turning new text into ids, applies that fixed list.

A common misreading is that encoding scans the vocabulary for the longest entry that matches, the way a dictionary lookup would. It does not, and the difference is observable. Encoding applies merges in learned rank order: rank 1 is the first merge learned, and a lower rank wins. It splits the input into base units, then repeatedly applies the earliest-learned merge that currently applies.

A long piece can therefore sit in the vocabulary and still never be produced for a given input. The merge sequence that would build it may not be reachable from that input's intermediate state. Ship a model with only the vocabulary and no merge ranks, and its tokenizer becomes unreproducible, even though every piece is present.

Encoding also never recounts anything from your prompt. If it did, the same word could tokenize differently on Tuesday, and every id addresses an embedding row (the learned vector stored for that id) that learned its value under one specific mapping. The merge list is part of the model contract, exactly as lesson 1.2 argued for the vocabulary.

## What does a real tokenizer do with the same word?

:::insight
Run the real thing next to your hand trace and the most instructive result is disagreement. Load Qwen3.8-27B's tokenizer, encode *lowest*, and you will very likely not get `low` plus `est`. Its merges were learned by counting pairs over whatever corpus the tokenizer was trained on, a corpus the model card never describes. So the pairs that won were the pairs frequent *there*, and the pieces reflect that corpus rather than English morphology. Watch for two more things while you are there. Leading spaces usually attach to the following piece rather than standing alone. And a deliberately strange input, such as an unusual emoji, will expand into several byte-derived pieces. Both behaviors follow directly from a byte-level base alphabet.
:::

Frequency creates efficiency and bias in the same motion. Strings common in the training corpus become single tokens and consume one position. Strings rare there remain long chains, however ordinary they are to their speakers. Capitalization, whitespace, and normalization all change which pairs were available to merge. The algorithm has no concept of a morpheme. It sometimes finds them only because a meaningful unit tends to be a reusable one.

## What does vocabulary size trade away?

Vocabulary size is the governing trade. More merges shorten common sequences but enlarge the embedding table and the output projection (the matrix that scores every vocabulary entry at the output). For Qwen3.8-27B that means 5120 numbers per row, twice over, since its input and output matrices are separate. Fewer merges shrink those matrices and lengthen every sequence, raising attention and decoding work. [Lesson 5.3](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.3-training-tokenizer/) revisits the choice from the training side; there is no universal token-to-word ratio to appeal to.

:::warning
It is tempting to read a BPE vocabulary as a list of meaningful units, so that an odd segmentation signals a defect. The pieces are compression artifacts of one corpus. What you should actually check is reproducibility. Keep these together: the normalization policy, the base alphabet, the ordered merge list, the special-token definitions, and the exact encoder implementation. Inspect real segmentations rather than trusting the algorithm's name, because two tokenizers labelled BPE can disagree on a string and both be correct implementations.
:::

The image to keep is a ladder of reusable chunks. Start with units that can spell any valid normalized Unicode string. Promote the most frequent adjacent combination one rung at a time. Keep a numbered log of the promotions so the climb can be repeated exactly. The ladder compresses surface patterns; what those pieces mean is still entirely the neural network's problem.
