Can your machine run Qwen3.8-27B? The honest answer is a ladder, not a yes or no — and the rung you land on is set by the quantization level you accept, using exactly the arithmetic of lessons 7.9 and 7.10.

At the top, **bf16**: 54 GB of weights (the course's budget math in lessons 7.2 and 9.3). That is data-center territory — an 80 GB-class accelerator, a multi-GPU split, or a very large unified-memory workstation with substantially more than 64 GB, and even then the fit is working, not comfortable. **Int8** lands near 27 GB: reachable on 32–48 GB accelerators or a 48 GB-plus unified-memory machine. **4-bit** artifacts run about 14–16 GB — the community GGUF Q4_K_M build sits near 16 GB — and this is the level the vendor means by its claim that the model runs in 24 GB of VRAM minimum. That claim deserves both of its qualifiers: it refers to the quantized model, not the model, and minimum means the door closes behind you, not that you have room to move.

The classic wrong turn shows why the qualifier matters. A buyer reasons: 27 billion parameters at 4 bits is $27\text{e}9 \times 0.5 = 13.5$ GB, so my 16 GB card works. Three corrections. First, the real artifact is about 16 GB, because scales and mixed-precision tensors ride along (lesson 7.10's effective-bits arithmetic). Second, weights are not the whole tenant: the KV cache, runtime workspace, and the operating system all claim memory. Third, unless the cache is quantized too, it accrues in bf16 regardless of weight precision (lesson 7.9's warning). The 16 GB card was never in the running.

Size the plausible setup instead — a 24 GB GPU, step by step. Weights: about 16 GB resident. Cache, from lesson 7.2's 64 KiB per token: an 8,192-token chat costs $8{,}192 \times 64\ \text{KiB} = 512\ \text{MiB}$; the Gated DeltaNet recurrent state adds a constant ~72 MiB per sequence. Runtime workspace, compute buffers, and display overhead: order of a few GB depending on backend. Total: roughly 19–20 GB — it fits, with modest headroom. Now stretch the context to 65,536 tokens: $65{,}536 \times 64\ \text{KiB} = 4\ \text{GiB}$ of cache, and the same card is brushing its ceiling. The native 262,144-token window is out of the question locally: 16 GiB of cache alone, rivaling the weights. A runtime letting you configure the maximum context is making a memory promise on your behalf — set the window to what you will actually use.

Capacity decides whether the model loads; **bandwidth** decides whether it is pleasant. Each decoded token moves a large fraction of the resident weight bytes through memory, so local generation speed tracks memory bandwidth far more than compute specs — one reason unified-memory machines punch above their apparent class, and the exact mathematics of lesson 9.1's roofline treatment. Remember too that this is a multimodal model: the vision tower is part of the checkpoint, and image inputs add encoder computation and buffers on top of text-only sizing.

Where does it actually run? The community quantization ecosystem for this model spans llama.cpp and its GGUF format, the runners that wrap it such as Ollama and LM Studio, and MLX on Apple silicon. One check outranks every download: the runtime must implement Qwen3.8-27B's hybrid architecture — Gated DeltaNet layers beside full attention — in its kernels. Support for brand-new layer types arrives in specific releases, not by osmosis; verify before pulling 16 GB. From here the course goes hands-on: lesson 8.7 works llama.cpp and GGUF properly, lesson 8.9 runs the model through MLX, and lesson 9.6 explains why Apple's unified memory changes the local calculus.

:::warning
Local does not automatically mean private or safe. Applications may send telemetry, plugins may read files, prompts can land in logs or swap, and community model files are third-party artifacts that can be tampered with. Verify sources and hashes, prefer non-executable tensor containers, and audit what your runner actually transmits.
:::

:::note
Benchmark your intended task, not a screenshot. Record runtime and model versions, backend, thread count, context length, and thermal state; measure load time, time to first token, tokens per second, and peak memory. A laptop that briefly bursts to a nice number may sustain something very different ten minutes into a session.
:::

The durable planning order: pick the quantization rung your memory affords; add cache at the context you will really use, plus state and workspace; confirm the runtime supports the architecture and consumes the packed format natively; then measure bandwidth-bound reality. Local inference is rewarding precisely because every layer is visible — but that also makes you the serving operator, and this lesson's arithmetic is your capacity plan.
