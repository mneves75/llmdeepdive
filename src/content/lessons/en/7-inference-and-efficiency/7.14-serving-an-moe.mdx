## Two numbers, moving in opposite directions

[Lesson 4.18](/lessons/4-transformer/4.18-mixture-of-experts/) ended with a model that stores 146.8 billion parameters and activates about 27 billion per token. Serving it means holding the first number and paying the second. A dense 27B model holds 54 GB in bf16; the MoE holds 293.6 GB, because every expert stays resident in case a token picks it, while a decoded token still reads a dense model's worth of weights.

That asymmetry decides the rest of this lesson. Memory is set by what is *stored*. Latency at batch one is set by what is *read*. Optimisations that shrink the first do not necessarily shrink the second, and the ones that do usually cost something else.

## Serving a smaller k

The trained model picks 8 experts per token. Nothing stops you from serving fewer: each dropped expert is compute saved, while every expert stays in memory for the tokens that still pick it.

The complication is the weights. A renormalising router divides the chosen weights by their sum, so the routed branch's gain is calibrated for the trained k. Serve top-4 and the four survivors share the same total, each with a larger weight than it had. That changes two things at once: which experts run, and how much each contributes.

Chen and Yao separate the two with one integer. Keep the top $k_1$ experts, but divide their weights by the softmax mass of the top $k_2$. Setting $k_2 = k_1$ is the usual renormalisation over the survivors; $k_2 = 8$ anchors the gain to the trained value; $k_2 = 16$ divides by a larger mass. On a Qwen3.6-35B-A3B router whose top 8 experts hold only 0.182 of the softmax mass, serving top-4 renormalised over the survivors lost 4.65 MMLU points against the trained model, dividing by the top-8 mass lost 3.10, and dividing by the top-16 mass lost 0.35 — a difference the authors report as not significant. Dropping renormalisation entirely at top-6 lost 27.45 points. In exchange, top-4 halved the activated routed parameters, from 1.006B to 0.503B, 17% of everything a token uses.

:::warning
Those numbers belong to one model with one flat router. On Gemma-4-26B-A4B, whose top 8 hold 0.302 of the mass, dropping renormalisation at top-6 raised perplexity by 0.7% against 356% on the flatter Qwen router. How much the denominator matters is a property of your router, and the way to learn it is to measure the top-k mass.
:::

Serving *more* experts than the model trained with is the other direction, and it does not reliably help: in one fine-tuning study, serving top-16 on a model trained at top-8 moved a nine-benchmark average from 41.35 to 41.09, inside the reported error bars. The extra combinations were never trained together.

## Low-bit experts

After training, the weights stop moving, and two numbers describe what they cost: the bytes in memory, and the bytes each decoded token reads. The routed experts hold most of the parameters, so their precision sets most of the model's size.

<Figure id="moe-serving-memory" />

For the model from lesson 4.18, the four steps show why the two numbers separate. Storing every weight in bf16 is 293.6 GB. Putting the routed experts in 4 bits takes that to 88.3 GB, a factor of 3.3. But a decoded token reads only its active weights: 17.1 billion active expert parameters, whose bill halves from 34.2 GB to 17.1 GB, plus the 9.9 billion non-expert parameters at bf16, about 19.8 GB, which do not shrink at all. Reads fall from 54 GB to 28.4 GB, a factor of 1.9. Quantizing attention as well takes storage to 73.4 GB and reads to 13.5 GB — a further quarter off the reads for only 15 GB of memory, because attention holds few parameters but every token reads it.

Shipped models take both scopes. gpt-oss-20b quantizes its MoE weights to about 4.25 bits per parameter; those weights hold more than 90% of the parameters, so the 120b model fits on one 80 GB GPU. Kimi K3 quantizes only its routed experts through SFT and RL, and ZAYA1-8B's release does the same. Qwen's GPTQ-Int4 build of its 30B MoE also quantizes attention.

The course's memory budget for one accelerator is [lesson 9.3](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.3-kv-cache-sizing-and-memory-budgets/). This lesson adds the MoE-specific term — the experts you must hold but a token may not read — and does not re-derive the KV cache.
:::

## Expert parallelism at serving

Training picks one EP size for one kind of step: a large batch of tokens processed together. Serving runs two kinds. A **prefill** step processes prompts and can carry tens of thousands of tokens per GPU. A **decode** step carries the next token or few of each request in flight, so each expert sees a small batch. Spreading experts over more GPUs helps decode: each GPU holds and reads fewer weights per step, while the all-to-all gathers a larger batch for each expert.

DeepSeek sizes the two in opposite directions for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash. Prefill runs at EP2 to EP8, the smallest size whose GPUs hold enough memory; decode runs at EP64 to EP128, the widest the available GPUs allow. With 64 requests per GPU, EP64 held 7 routed experts per GPU and decoded about 15,700 tokens per second per GPU at about 246 per user, against EP8's 52 experts and about 12,600 at 198 — about 25% higher on both. At 256 requests per GPU the two nearly met.

The price is structural. Prefill and decode get roughly equal GPUs, so a deployment at decode EP64 has a floor of 128 GPUs. Every GPU in one EP instance is also a single communication domain: one failed GPU takes the whole instance down, and on restart the group is re-sized to the healthy GPUs.

:::caveat
Under expert parallelism, a step runs as a CUDA graph only when every EP rank is decoding. If any rank has a prefill in its batch, all ranks fall back to eager mode. A mixed workload therefore loses the graph on the steps where a prefill lands, which is a scheduling question, not a kernel one.
:::

## Attention and FFN in separate pools

In a colocated layout each GPU runs both halves of each layer. Decoding makes different demands on them: attention holds few parameters but keeps the key-value cache of every request in flight, while the experts hold most of the parameters and keep no per-request state. **Attention–FFN disaggregation** puts them on separate GPUs and passes micro-batches between the pools, so each pool computes while the other works.

MegaScale-Infer reports up to 1.90 times the per-GPU throughput of earlier serving systems. For a specific deployment, FastAFD runs Qwen3-235B-A22B on 28 attention GPUs plus 4 expert GPUs and reports 1.41 times the per-GPU throughput of colocated EP4. That comparison is the one to examine: when a later study re-provisioned both layouts for the same 72-GPU budget, the ratio fell to 0.99. Across all its modelled settings, homogeneous disaggregation raised fixed-budget throughput in only a minority.

Training also constrains this. Step-3 derives the sparsest MoE an expert pool can serve while staying compute-bound and overlapping communication, and the bound rises with width and depth: about 0.058 for DeepSeek-V3's width and depth on H800, with Step-3 itself at about 0.08. A sparser model has to fall back on large expert parallelism or on routing restrictions.

## The routing mismatch in RL

Reinforcement learning separates sample generation from gradient computation. A rollout engine generates with its own kernels and, in off-policy or asynchronous RL, with weights an update or more behind the trainer's. These differences shift probabilities; in an MoE they can also change which experts a token runs through, so the trainer differentiates a path the sample never took.

Both causes have been measured. Generating with one engine and training with another, about 10% of routers on Qwen3-30B-A3B chose different experts, and 94% of tokens differed in at least one layer. After one gradient update, about 10% of the activated experts for the same rollout had changed. The two 10%s count different things — engines that disagree, and experts that move with the weights.

Recording the rollout's expert choices and replaying them in the trainer removes the first cause: the train-inference KL fell from 1.5e-3 to 7.5e-4, close to a dense model's 6.4e-4. Whether it improves reward is contested: the authors report stability gains, a later Qwen study saw none in on-policy training, and another found replay below plain GRPO on one model but not on another. The GSPO authors, who had used replay, dropped it for sequence-level importance ratios.

:::note
A separate lever targets the kernels' own share of the mismatch: **batch-invariant kernels** make a token's log-probability independent of how requests were batched. Megatron documents this as a fix for "a real source of error and instability in RL" and warns that it significantly affects speed; DeepSeek's DeepInfer ships the same option.
:::

## What this does not establish

- The 146.8B model is the course's counterfactual. Its memory arithmetic is exact; no serving benchmark of it exists.
- The MMLU and throughput figures belong to the models and budgets named. The equal-budget re-provisioning that turned 1.41 into 0.99 is the caution, not an exception.
- Replay's reward effect is not settled, and the sources disagree in public.
- None of this says an MoE is cheaper to serve than a dense model of the same quality. It says where the costs sit, and which lever moves which one.
