You have a token's hidden state: 5120 numbers, as lesson 0.4 established. Inside every layer of Qwen3.8-27B, that state gets pushed through a feed-forward block that first widens it to 17408 dimensions and then narrows it back. Both of those numbers come straight out of the model's configuration. The question this lesson answers is what that widening actually *does*, and what it costs, because the answer explains most of where a 27-billion-parameter model's parameters and arithmetic actually go.

Matrix multiplication is easiest to understand as a transformation rather than as a grid-filling ritual. A matrix $W$ takes coordinates in one space and produces coordinates in another. Each output component of $Wx$ is a dot product between one row of $W$ and the input $x$. The rows are therefore independent learned questions asked about the same input, and the number of rows is how many questions get asked.

A small case makes the geometry visible. Take

$$
W=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1/2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Applied to $(x_1,x_2)$ it yields $(2x_1, x_2/2)$: a square becomes a rectangle, stretched horizontally and squashed vertically. Other matrices rotate, reflect, shear, or project onto a subspace. At 5120 dimensions the picture disappears, but the operation is unchanged — a learned change of representation, nothing more exotic.

Now the real one. Qwen3.8-27B's up-projection has shape $5120 \times 17408$. To transform a single token, the machine must produce 17408 output coordinates, and each one is a dot product over 5120 inputs. Count it directly:

$$
5120 \times 17408 = 89{,}128{,}960 \approx 89.1\ \text{million multiply-adds}.
$$

That is one matrix, one layer, one token. The model has 64 layers, and the feed-forward block of each layer contains more than one matrix of comparable size; lesson 4.9 does the full accounting and shows that the feed-forward blocks hold the large majority of the model's parameters. But hold the single number first, because it recalibrates intuition. Generating a hundred-token reply is not a hundred small lookups. Every token pushed through every layer triggers tens of millions of multiply-adds in this matrix alone, and the hardware questions of track 9 exist because of it.

:::insight
Notice what the widening does not do. Going from 5120 coordinates to 17408 cannot create independent information; the output still lies on an image of a 5120-dimensional space, so at most 5120 independent directions survive. What the wider space buys is *room*, so that a nonlinearity applied there can carve up the representation in ways that are impossible in the narrow one. The expansion followed by a nonlinear function followed by a contraction is the entire idea of a feed-forward block.
:::

The **rank** of a matrix measures how many independent directions actually pass through it. A low-rank map forces its outputs into a smaller subspace than the shape suggests. This is not merely a caveat: it is the mechanism behind parameter-efficient fine-tuning in lesson 6.3, which adapts a huge matrix by adding a deliberately low-rank correction rather than retraining all 89 million numbers.

Matrix-matrix multiplication does many of these transformations at once. Entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. More important than the recipe is the meaning: $AB$ represents **composition**, with $B$ acting first and $A$ second. Multiplication is not commutative. $AB$ and $BA$ can differ, and one can be defined while the other is not. Shape is what records which compositions make sense.

Neural layers usually compute $y = Wx + b$. The matrix supplies the linear transformation and the **bias** translates every result, making the map affine rather than strictly linear: zero can now map to $b$ instead of to zero. That freedom lets a decision boundary sit somewhere other than through the origin.

Here is the classic wrong turn, and it is the reason activation functions exist at all. Suppose you stack two linear layers to get depth: $W_2(W_1 x)$. By associativity that equals $(W_2 W_1)x$, and $W_2 W_1$ is just another matrix. Two layers have exactly the expressive power of one. Add biases and the same collapse happens with a single combined affine map. Stack fifty of them and you still have one matrix. Depth in a purely linear network is an illusion, purchased with parameters and paid for in arithmetic, delivering nothing. Something nonlinear must interrupt the composition, which in Qwen's feed-forward block is a gated activation covered in lesson 2.3.

:::warning
The misconception to name explicitly is that a bigger matrix means a smarter layer. Size buys capacity, not capability. A wider projection with no nonlinearity after it composes away to nothing; a full-rank matrix that maps every input to nearly the same output direction is expensive and useless. What matters is what survives the transformation and what the following nonlinearity can then do with it, which is why reading model code means reading shapes *and* what sits between them.
:::

Matrix multiplication dominates neural computation partly because it is expressive and largely because hardware is exceptionally good at it. A batch of token vectors multiplied by one weight matrix reuses the same parameters across every position, so the arithmetic-to-memory ratio is favorable. Accelerators split large products into tiles, stream them through a memory hierarchy, and accumulate partial sums. The algebra is simple; whether it runs fast depends on shapes, precision, data movement, and fusion, which is the subject of tracks 8 and 9.

The reading habit to take forward is a question asked at every matrix in a model: which space enters, which space leaves, how many independent directions can survive, and what does this cost per token? Applied to Qwen's 5120 by 17408 up-projection, the answers are a narrow state in, a wide state out, at most 5120 independent directions, and 89.1 million multiply-adds. That is what a transformer layer feels like from the inside.
