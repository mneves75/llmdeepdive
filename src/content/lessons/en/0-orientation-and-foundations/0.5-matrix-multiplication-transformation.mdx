You have a token's hidden state: 5120 numbers, as [lesson 0.4](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.4-vectors-matrices-dot-products/) established. Inside every layer of Qwen3.8-27B, that state goes through a feed-forward block, the part of each layer that transforms every token on its own ([lesson 0.1](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.1-what-is-a-language-model/)). The block first widens the state to 17408 dimensions and then narrows it back. Both of those numbers come straight out of the model's configuration.

This lesson answers two questions: what does that widening actually *do*, and what does it cost? The answers explain most of where a 27-billion-parameter model's parameters and arithmetic go.

## A matrix is a transformation

Matrix multiplication is easiest to understand as a transformation rather than as a grid-filling ritual. A matrix $W$ takes coordinates in one space and produces coordinates in another. Each output component of $Wx$ is a dot product between one row of $W$ and the input $x$. The rows are therefore independent learned questions asked about the same input, and the number of rows is how many questions get asked.

A small case makes the geometry visible. Take

$$
W=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1/2\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Applied to $(x_1,x_2)$ it yields $(2x_1, x_2/2)$: a square becomes a rectangle, stretched horizontally and squashed vertically. Other matrices rotate, reflect, shear (slant), or project onto a smaller space. At 5120 dimensions the picture disappears, but the operation is unchanged: a learned change of representation, nothing more exotic.

## What one real matrix costs

Now the real one. With the convention used here, $Wx$, Qwen3.8-27B's up-projection (the widening matrix) has shape $17408 \times 5120$: one row for each output and one column for each input. To transform a single token, the machine must produce 17408 output coordinates, and each one is a dot product over 5120 inputs. Each pair in a dot product costs one **multiply-add**: multiply two numbers, add the result to a running sum. Count them directly:

$$
5120 \times 17408 = 89{,}128{,}960 \approx 89.1\ \text{million multiply-adds}.
$$

That is one matrix, one layer, one token. The model has 64 layers, and the feed-forward block of each layer contains more than one matrix of comparable size. [Lesson 4.9](/lessons/4-transformer/4.9-feed-forward-block/) does the full accounting and shows that the feed-forward blocks hold the large majority of the model's parameters.

But hold the single number first, because it recalibrates intuition. Generating a hundred-token reply is not a hundred small lookups. Every token pushed through every layer triggers tens of millions of multiply-adds in this matrix alone, and the hardware questions of track 9 exist because of it.

:::insight
Notice what the widening does not do. Going from 5120 coordinates to 17408 cannot create independent information. The 17408 outputs are a linear function of the same 5120 inputs, so together they span at most 5120 independent directions. What the wider space buys is *room*. A **nonlinearity**, a function whose graph is not a straight line, applied there can carve up the representation in ways that are impossible in the narrow one. Expansion, then a nonlinear function, then contraction: that is the entire idea of a feed-forward block.
:::

## Rank: how many directions survive

The **rank** of a matrix measures how many independent directions actually pass through it. A low-rank map forces its outputs into a smaller space than its shape suggests. Rank is also the mechanism behind the parameter-efficient fine-tuning of [lesson 6.3](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.3-lora-qlora-and-peft/). That method adapts a huge matrix by adding a deliberately low-rank correction rather than retraining all 89 million numbers.

## Composition: multiplying two matrices

Matrix-matrix multiplication does many of these transformations at once. Entry $(i,j)$ of $AB$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. More important than the recipe is the meaning: $AB$ represents **composition**, with $B$ acting first and $A$ second, the way `f(g(x))` runs `g` first.

Multiplication of matrices is not commutative: the order matters. $AB$ and $BA$ can differ, and one can be defined while the other is not. Shape is what records which compositions make sense.

Neural layers usually compute $y = Wx + b$. The matrix supplies the linear transformation and the **bias** shifts every result by the same vector. That makes the map **affine** rather than strictly linear: zero can now map to $b$ instead of to zero. The freedom lets a decision boundary, the line where the layer's answer flips, sit somewhere other than through the origin.

## Why stacked linear layers collapse into one

Here is the classic wrong turn, and it is the reason activation functions exist at all. Suppose you stack two linear layers to get depth: $W_2(W_1 x)$. By associativity that equals $(W_2 W_1)x$, and $W_2 W_1$ is just another matrix. Two layers have exactly the expressive power of one. Add biases and the same collapse happens with a single combined affine map. Stack fifty of them and you still have one matrix.

Depth in a purely linear network is an illusion: you pay for it in parameters and arithmetic, and it delivers nothing. Something nonlinear must interrupt the composition. In Qwen's feed-forward block that is a gated activation, covered in [lesson 2.3](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.3-activation-functions/).

:::warning
The misconception to name explicitly is that a bigger matrix means a smarter layer. Size buys capacity, not capability. A wider projection with no nonlinearity after it composes away to nothing; a full-rank matrix that maps every input to nearly the same output direction is expensive and useless. What matters is what survives the transformation and what the following nonlinearity can then do with it, which is why reading model code means reading shapes *and* what sits between them.
:::

## Why hardware is built around matrix multiplication

Matrix multiplication dominates neural computation partly because it is expressive and largely because hardware is exceptionally good at it. A batch of token vectors multiplied by one weight matrix reuses the same parameters across every position, so each number loaded from memory feeds many calculations. Accelerators split large products into tiles, stream them through a memory hierarchy, and accumulate partial sums. The algebra is simple. Whether it runs fast depends on shapes, precision, data movement, and fusion (merging several operations into one pass), which is the subject of tracks 8 and 9.

The reading habit to take forward is a question asked at every matrix in a model: which space enters, which space leaves, how many independent directions can survive, and what does this cost per token? Applied to Qwen's projection from 5120 inputs to 17408 outputs, the answers are a narrow state in, a wide state out, at most 5120 independent directions, and 89.1 million multiply-adds. That is what a transformer layer feels like from the inside.
