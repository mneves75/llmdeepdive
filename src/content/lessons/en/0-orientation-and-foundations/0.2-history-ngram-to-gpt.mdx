Every language model in this course answers the same question: given the text so far, what comes next? What changed over sixty years is *how much* of the text so far a model could use before its estimates or its computation fell apart. This lesson follows that one problem through four machines: counting, learned vectors, recurrence, and attention.

## Counting: the trigram model

Suppose it is 1990 and you have a large collection of English text, a **corpus**, and a simple goal: predict the next word. The obvious plan is to count. Take the two preceding words as context, look up how often each word followed that pair in your corpus, and divide. This is a **trigram model** (three words: two of context, one predicted). It is a perfectly respectable language model in the sense of [lesson 0.1](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.1-what-is-a-language-model/): it returns a probability distribution over the vocabulary given a context.

Work an example. Suppose your corpus contains the pair *butter and* eighty times, followed by *jelly* fifty-two times, *jam* nine times, *sugar* four times, and fifteen other continuations once each. The estimate for jelly is $52/80 = 0.65$, for jam $9/80 \approx 0.11$, and so on. Counting and dividing like this is called the **maximum-likelihood estimate**. The distribution is honest, inspectable, and required no training run. So far the counting approach looks not merely adequate but attractive.

## Where counting breaks

Now give it the context *quantum peanut*. Your corpus has never contained that pair. The maximum-likelihood estimate tries to divide each zero continuation count by a zero context count: $0/0$, which is undefined, so no next-word distribution exists.

A related failure occurs when the context was observed but a particular continuation was not. That continuation receives probability zero, and one zero factor wipes out the probability of any sequence containing it. Two repairs exist. **Smoothing** reserves some probability for unseen events, which fixes both cases. **Backoff** uses the shorter context *peanut* when the longer one is missing. Production speech recognizers ran on smoothed n-grams for two decades.

But smoothing patches the symptom. The deeper defect is that the table cannot generalize sideways. A trigram model uses only the last two words as context, so *the cat sat on the* and *the dog sat on the* collapse to the same key, *on the*: the model treats the two sentences as identical and cannot tell that the animal changed. Nothing in the representation says the two contexts are almost the same, and nothing says they differ either — the earlier words were discarded.

And the table grows exponentially with the context length. With a vocabulary of a hundred thousand words, a four-gram table has $10^{20}$ conceivable entries, of which your corpus fills a vanishing fraction. A larger context meant an exponentially emptier table. That is the wall.

## Learned vectors let similar words share evidence

Bengio and colleagues got past the wall in 2003 by replacing the table with learned vectors. Each vocabulary item gets a list of numbers, its **embedding**, and a neural network maps a window of those embeddings to a distribution. Because *cat* and *dog* can be assigned nearby vectors, evidence gathered about one transfers to the other automatically, without anybody writing a rule.

This paper also fixed the shape of everything that followed: embeddings in, a function with learned parameters in the middle, logits (raw scores, lesson 0.1) over the vocabulary, softmax, and training by maximizing the probability of the observed text. Every model in this course, Qwen included, still has exactly that silhouette.

## Recurrence: no fixed window, but one step at a time

Recurrent networks (RNNs) then removed the fixed window, at least on paper. At each step an RNN mixes the current token with a **hidden state**, a vector carried over from the previous step. That state can in principle summarize any amount of the text before it.

In practice, training struggles across long chains of updates. Gradients, the signals that tell each parameter which way to move ([lesson 0.6](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.6-derivatives-gradients/)), shrink or explode as they pass back through many steps, so the early parts of the summary decay. LSTM and GRU cells added gates that decide what to keep and what to overwrite, which helped a great deal.

What could not be fixed was the sequencing. Step twenty needs the state from step nineteen, so the steps cannot run side by side, and training could not use a large machine efficiently.

## Attention: every position, all at once

Attention arrived first as a repair inside machine translation. There an **encoder** reads the source sentence and a **decoder** writes the translation. Attention let the decoder consult many encoder states instead of compressing the whole source sentence into one vector.

The 2017 Transformer promoted attention to the central operation and deleted recurrence entirely. Every position forms queries, keys, and values, three vectors that [lesson 4.2](/lessons/4-transformer/4.2-self-attention/) builds from scratch. Every position can consult every earlier position. And because the whole thing is matrix multiplication, training runs across all positions at once. **Causal masking**, which hides every later position from each position, keeps a language model from peeking at the answers even while all positions compute in parallel.

:::warning
The tempting misreading of this history is that each era replaced the previous objective. It did not. Counting, recurrence, and attention are three ways to compute the same thing: a distribution over the next token conditioned on what came before. Confusing a change of mechanism with a change of goal makes modern architectures look mysterious when they are cheaper answers to the 1990 question.
:::

## Scale, and where Qwen3.8-27B fits

Scaling did the rest. Parameters, data, context length, and training infrastructure all grew. Then **post-training**, extra training after the main run, turned a raw predictor into something that behaves like an assistant; two common methods are instruction tuning and preference optimization. Labels such as *the GPT-5 era* are marketing chronology, not scientific boundaries. Deployed products bundle models with routers, tools, safety layers, and caches, and vendors disclose different amounts of it.

Which brings the story to where this course picks it up. In August 2026 the Qwen team announced the Qwen3.8 family. Its Qwen3.8-27B is published under Apache 2.0: a dense model of roughly 27 billion parameters that accepts text, images, and video and emits text, with a native context of 262,144 tokens. Two of its properties matter for this history.

- It is **multimodal**, so the sequence it attends over is no longer purely linguistic.
- It is **hybrid**. Rather than 64 identical attention blocks, it interleaves full attention with a recurrent-state mechanism called Gated DeltaNet, a direct descendant of the RNN idea that attention supposedly retired.

The wheel did not turn backwards. The recurrent state came back because attention's cost grows with context and a fixed-size state's does not. At a quarter of a million tokens that difference decides what can be served at all. [Lesson 4.16](/lessons/4-transformer/4.16-hybrid-layouts/) tells that story properly.

There is no dedicated technical report for this model, only a model card. [Lesson 0.8](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.8-how-to-read-ml-paper/) treats that absence as its subject rather than an inconvenience.
