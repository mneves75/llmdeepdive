You type *The capital of France is* into a chat box and a word comes back: Paris. Before any theory, ask a blunt engineering question about that word. Where, physically, did it come from?

Two answers feel obvious and both are wrong. It was not looked up in a table of questions and answers: nobody wrote that table, and the same system answers questions nobody anticipated. It was not decided by a small person living in the datacenter either. What actually happened is narrower, stranger, and completely inspectable. This course spends the next hundred-plus lessons opening it up, and this lesson gives you the outline.

## What happens between your prompt and "Paris"?

Follow the request through, one step at a time.

1. The text becomes **tokens**. A token is a unit from a fixed vocabulary: not a letter, and not quite a word. [Lesson 1.2](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.2-tokenization-i/) unpacks how text is cut into them.
2. Each token becomes an integer **id**, its position in that vocabulary, the way a dictionary key maps to an index.
3. Each id becomes a **vector**, a list of numbers, and those vectors pass through the network.
4. At the end, one final matrix produces one raw score for every entry in the output, for the position we are predicting. These raw scores are called **logits**. They can be negative or large, and they do not add up to anything in particular.
5. A function called **softmax** converts the logits into probabilities: numbers that are non-negative and sum to exactly one. [Lesson 0.3](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.3-probability-you-need/) shows how.

That list of probabilities is the output. Perhaps the token for Paris receives most of the probability, a punctuation token receives a little, and other candidates split what is left (the next section explains which output entries a runtime can actually select). Then, and only then, a separate rule picks one token. One complete run of the network from input to logits is called a **forward pass**.

:::caveat
Take any specific probability quoted in this lesson as illustrative. The output width of 248,320 is a published fact about Qwen3.8-27B; the exact probability it puts on any particular token is not, and we never invent measurements in this course.
:::

## Why the course follows one real model

Throughout, we will not talk about language models in general when we can talk about one. Our specimen is **Qwen3.8-27B**, from the Qwen3.8 family the Qwen team announced in August 2026, published under the Apache 2.0 licence, with published weights anyone can download and open. Choosing a real model means every claim has a place to be checked, and the numbers are specific rather than round.

Here is the first and most important one. Qwen3.8-27B is a function that takes a context and returns 248,320 logits for the next position, one per row of its output matrix. A second component, the **tokenizer**, converts between text and ids, and it maps 248,077 ids. The two numbers differ: 243 output rows have no tokenizer mapping, so no text corresponds to them. A **serving runtime**, the program that runs the model for users, turns the relevant logits into a probability distribution over the next tokens it is allowed to select. The published artifacts do not specify how every runtime handles those 243 unmapped rows.

That is the whole language-model contract, made concrete: context in, one score per output row out. Keep the shape of the output (248,320 rows) separate from the tokenizer's mapping (248,077 ids), because later lessons depend on the difference.

## The model scores; a separate rule chooses

Beginners routinely say the model outputs a word. It does not. It outputs about a quarter of a million numbers, and the choice among them is made afterwards by a **decoding rule** you control. The distinction has a visible consequence: the same model, given the same prompt twice, can answer differently.

Qwen3.8-27B ships with two official sampling presets. One uses temperature 1.0, for its thinking mode; the other uses temperature 0.7, for its instruct mode. Swapping between them changes the output without changing a single parameter of the model. Lesson 0.3 explains what temperature does to a distribution, and [lesson 7.4](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.4-sampling/) makes the presets concrete.

## The textbook block and the shipped model

One framing choice governs the whole course, and it is easier to state now than to discover later. Almost every textbook, diagram, and tutorial teaches the **transformer**, the network design behind modern language models, as a stack of identical blocks. Each block has two parts: **attention**, which lets each position gather information from other positions, then a **feed-forward network**, which transforms each position on its own. The pair repeats N times.

That uniform block is the **pedagogical baseline**. It is the right thing to learn first, because everything else is described as a departure from it. Qwen3.8-27B is the **shipped instance**, and shipped instances modify the baseline for reasons of cost, memory, or hardware. Its 64 layers are not identical: only one layer in four is the classic attention block you will study in track 4.

The Signal Observatory on the explore page animates the baseline, not the instance. When the two disagree, the Observatory is showing you the idea and the model is showing you the engineering. Each lesson will say which one it is talking about.

## Does the model learn from what you type?

Training and inference are different phases, and keeping them apart from the start prevents a lot of confusion.

- **Training** happened before release. The parameters, the numbers inside the model's matrices, were adjusted so that text actually observed in the training data received higher probability than text that did not.
- **Inference** is everything you experience as a user. The parameters are frozen during it.

So nothing you type teaches the model anything. The apparent learning inside a conversation comes entirely from the growing context being fed back in at every step. That is why the size of the context, 262,144 tokens natively for this model, is an architectural headline rather than a footnote.

## What a probability from the model does and does not tell you

The knowledge lives spread across the weights rather than in documents that can be retrieved. So the question *which page did this answer come from?* usually has no answer. Verbatim memorization does happen, especially for distinctive or heavily repeated passages, and it matters for privacy and for honest evaluation. But a useful model also continues combinations of ideas that never appeared together anywhere, and no index of sources could produce that.

:::warning
The most common misconception about language models is that a high probability is a claim of truth. It is not. Training rewarded predicting text that resembles the training data, not consulting reality. A widespread misconception is, by construction, probable text. Retrieval, calculators, code execution, and verification can raise reliability, but they are machinery bolted around the model; none of them changes what the forward pass computes.
:::

Whether such a system *understands* is a question this course will not settle, and you should be suspicious of anyone who settles it quickly in either direction. What we can say precisely is this: it builds internal representations that depend on context, those representations support a startling range of tasks, and its failures have a characteristic shape unlike a confident human speaker's.

To put the pieces together: Qwen3.8-27B is a learned probability engine over token sequences, with 248,077 mapped token ids, 248,320 output rows, and a context of a quarter-million positions. Conversation, translation, summarization, and code generation are behaviors assembled from that one operation, repeated.
