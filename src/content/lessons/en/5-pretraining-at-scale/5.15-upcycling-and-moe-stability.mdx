## Where the weights start

An MoE can start from random weights, like any model, or from a dense checkpoint whose feed-forward blocks already work. The second option is **upcycling**: cut each dense FFN into pieces, copy the pieces into experts, and keep training.

[Lesson 4.18](/lessons/4-transformer/4.18-mixture-of-experts/) chose a shape where the pieces fit exactly. The dense FFN is 17,408 wide and the experts are 2,176 wide, so one dense block splits into eight slices, and each slice is copied into eight of the 64 experts. At initialization the MoE holds the dense block's knowledge: the eight slices together cover the old width, provided the router draws one expert from each group of eight.

<Figure id="moe-upcycle-slices" />

Two details keep the copy honest. The optimizer state does not carry over — Megatron saves the freshly upcycled MoE at iteration 1 with no optimizer state — so the MoE begins a new schedule. And Megatron rescales the copied weights, because a token's eight experts now *sum* into what one wider block used to compute; the scaling factor depends on the activation (a cube root for a gated FFN, a square root for a plain ReLU one). Read the rescaling as a correction for summing copies, not as a hyperparameter to tune.

:::note
An MoE with one expert and top-1 computes exactly the dense function, which makes a parity run possible: upcycle the dense checkpoint, train the one-expert MoE beside the dense model, and the two losses should track. The handbook reports a gap of 0.00087 over 15 aligned steps in its run. That is a test of the plumbing, not a result about MoE quality.
:::

## What the head start is worth

The head start is the compute already spent on the dense run. A run from scratch eventually catches up, and how soon decides whether upcycling was worth it.

In OLMoE, a from-scratch MoE caught up with the upcycled one after 500B tokens, a quarter of the 2T tokens the dense model had trained on. In Komatsuzaki et al.'s sparse-upcycling runs, catching up took 120% of the dense checkpoint's compute — more than simply training the dense model had cost. So the trade is: upcycle when the MoE run is short relative to the dense run, and expect a long run to pay the whole bill anyway.

The same idea applies to growing an existing MoE: copy experts and their router rows to go from 32 to 64. An unperturbed copy ties with its original, so a token splits its weight between two identical functions and gains nothing. Perturbing the copy breaks the tie and leaves the router something to learn; fresh random experts avoid the tie but start untrained. Both have been used, and the handbook names which model used which.

## The optimizer sees a different problem

Each expert sees only the tokens routed to it. In a batch of millions of tokens, a rare expert might see a handful. That asymmetry is where AdamW and Muon behave differently.

**AdamW** divides each weight's update by that weight's own gradient history. An expert with few tokens produces a thin, noisy gradient, and its step shrinks accordingly. **Muon** first orthogonalizes the update, levelling its nonzero singular values to one, then applies a factor determined only by the matrix's dimensions. The step size no longer follows the gradient's magnitude, and momentum carries the expert through a step in which it received no tokens at all.

Moonlight — 3B active, 16B total — beat its AdamW twin by a clear margin under identical settings at about 1.2T tokens. Two cautions come with it. Under Muon, experts can collapse, and too large an update scale can make a run diverge; the handbook reports that Megatron's default scale of 1.0 diverged on a dense 7B in one team's tests, and that starting at 0.2 was safer.

The router is a separate question that adopters answer differently. Practice splits here: the handbook reports DeepSeek-V4 leaving its router on Muon, while Qwen3.8-Next switched it to AdamW once early Muon updates made routing unstable. A small comparison found AdamW on the router slightly worse than row-normalized or left-spectral updates. There is no settled answer; if you adopt Muon, decide the router's optimizer deliberately rather than by inheritance.

## Multi-token prediction

**Multi-token prediction** (MTP) adds one transformer layer per extra token to predict. The extra layer takes the model's hidden state together with the *next* token's embedding, projects the concatenation back to the hidden width, and predicts the token after next through the same output head the main model uses. DeepSeek-V3 reports gains on most benchmarks at two scales, and the added depth can be removed before serving.

It costs step time, and the cost lands in one place. A pipeline step's pace is set by its slowest stage, so the added layer's cost is its share of that one stage's work — far more than its share of the model. Absent a custom layout, Megatron assigns the MTP layer to the final stage, where the output head already sits. The handbook reports early steps about 37% longer with one MTP layer on its 256-node runs, and spreading the model over eight pipeline stages did not close the gap; a 14B MoE elsewhere reports 7%. DeepSeek-V3 weighted the MTP loss at 0.3 for its first 10T tokens and 0.1 after.

## Three quiet failures

A run can fail loudly or quietly. The loud failure is a NaN or an infinite value in the loss or the gradients. Megatron checks every step by default, reruns the step on the same GPU to separate a transient error from a repeatable one, and if the NaN repeats, exits with code 16 so the job restarts from a checkpoint on another GPU. A second rerun there separates a faulty GPU from a NaN the data or the model really produced. A **spiky loss** — ten times the largest of the first hundred values — gets the same treatment without stopping the run.

The quiet failures are the ones that cost days, because the loss can stay flat through all three:

| Failure | What moves | What you read |
|---|---|---|
| Routing collapse | Tokens pile onto a few experts | MaxVio climbs, per layer |
| Dead expert | One expert's output shrinks toward zero | Its mean output norm far below the layer's median |
| Rogue expert | One expert's output grows without bound | Its mean output norm far above the median |

The rogue case is not hypothetical: the handbook reports one expert in Step 3.5 Flash's layer 45 reaching about 5,700 times the median output norm while the loss barely moved. That is why the norm ratio, not the loss, is the signal, and why some models clamp the activation before it can grow.

:::caveat
None of these measurements is on by default in every framework, and each requires its own capture: token counts per expert for MaxVio, an activation dump or a forward hook for the norms. A run without them can look healthy for its whole duration. Budget the monitoring before the run, not after the first suspicious plateau.
:::

## What this does not establish

- The catch-up points (500B tokens, 120% of compute) come from two specific comparisons. Another dense checkpoint and another data mix move them.
- Muon's advantage is measured on one model shape at one scale. Its interaction with the router is contested between shipped models.
- The MTP cost figures are step-time shares under specific pipeline layouts, not a general price.
- Nothing here claims an MoE trains more stably than a dense model. It claims the failure modes differ, and that the signals must differ too.
