Someone will ask you what it cost to train Qwen3.8-27B. The honest answer has two parts, and both matter: nobody outside the team knows, and here is how you would estimate it by supplying the missing inputs yourself. This lesson builds that estimate end to end and labels every assumption along the way, so that what comes out is a method you can apply rather than a figure you can quote.

## How much arithmetic does training take?

For a dense decoder (a left-to-right model that runs every token through all of its layers' weights, with no routing to selected experts), the common Kaplan first-order shortcut for the model core, from [lesson 5.4](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.4-kaplan-scaling-laws/), is

$$
F_{core}\approx 6N_{core}D,
$$

with $N_{core}$ the non-embedding parameters and $D$ the training tokens. The factor of six approximates the matrix work of the forward and backward passes: roughly two operations per parameter per token forward, and twice that again backward. This shortcut estimates the core. It does not say that every operation outside $N_{core}$ is free.

Start with the rough version people often produce. Take the marketed 27 billion parameters, choose 4 trillion tokens as a round illustrative input, and multiply:

$$
6\times(27\times 10^{9})\times(4\times 10^{12})=6.48\times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs}.
$$

The shortcut is meant to use the non-embedding count instead. [Lesson 5.3](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.3-training-tokenizer/) showed that the 248,320-row input embedding and its untied output head together hold about 2.54 billion parameters, and there is a vision tower besides. Using about 24.5 billion for $N_{core}$ gives

$$
6\times(2.45\times 10^{10})\times(4\times 10^{12})=5.88\times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs},
$$

That 5.88e23 figure is the core approximation, not a better total. The untied output head still multiplies every final hidden state by a $248{,}320\times5{,}120$ matrix to produce logits (the raw score for every vocabulary token). Chinchilla's Appendix F counts that final logits projection separately. The head has about 1.27 billion weights, so under the same forward-plus-backward convention its contribution is

$$
6\times(1.2714\times 10^{9})\times(4\times 10^{12})\approx3.05\times 10^{22}\ \text{FLOPs}.
$$

Adding the core shortcut and the head gives about $6.19\times10^{23}$ FLOPs before architecture-specific corrections. The input embedding is different: it is a table lookup, not a matrix multiplication, so treating it as another dense projection over the full vocabulary would also be wrong. Counting the actual operators is what separates these costs.

## Why is the token count the biggest unknown?

The largest uncertainty is $D$. **There is no published token count for this model.** No technical report exists, and the model card does not disclose training tokens, training compute, utilization, hardware, or cost. The 4 trillion above is a round assumption used only to demonstrate the method; it is not a claim about Qwen3.8-27B. Change the assumption to 2 trillion and every number below halves.

A further caveat sits underneath the shortcut. Here, 48 of the 64 layers are Gated DeltaNet rather than full attention, which changes the operation count per token and how cost grows with sequence length. Treat $6.19\times10^{23}$ as a first-order construction, not a measurement; a precise estimate would count the actual operators in every path.

## How do FLOPs become device-hours and money?

Now convert work into time. A device-hour is one accelerator running for one hour. Realized device-hours are

$$
H=\frac{F_{train}}{P_{peak}\,u\,3600},
$$

where $P_{peak}$ is the applicable peak number of operations per second in the dtype actually used, $u$ is the measured utilization (the model FLOPs utilization of [lesson 5.8](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.8-model-parallelism/)), and 3600 converts seconds to hours. For a worked estimate, assume an order-of-magnitude $10^{15}$ bf16 FLOP/s and 40% model FLOPs utilization. Those are illustrative inputs, not facts about Qwen's hardware or run. With them:

$$
H=\frac{6.19\times 10^{23}}{10^{15}\times 0.40\times 3600}\approx 430{,}000\ \text{device-hours}.
$$

On 1,024 accelerators that is about 420 hours, or 17.5 days of continuous training. At an illustrative rented rate of USD 2.50 per device-hour (a figure that must carry its date, region, and purchasing model), the final run costs roughly

$$
430{,}000\times 2.50 = \text{USD } 1{,}075{,}000.
$$

That is one line of the budget, and it is the line everyone quotes as though it were the total.

## What does the final-run figure leave out?

:::warning
The most common way a training-cost figure misleads is its boundary, not its arithmetic. "The final run cost about a million" and "the model cost about a million to develop" are different claims, and only the first follows from the chain above. Scaling pilots, data ablations (experiments that remove or swap one data source to measure its effect), tokenizer experiments, abandoned architectures, failed runs, rolled-back steps from the incidents of [lesson 5.12](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.12-training-instabilities/), evaluation, and post-training are all real device-hours. Applying an illustrative program multiplier of 1.5 to 3 times the final run would put this example between about USD 1.6 million and USD 3.2 million before any non-compute line at all. The multiplier is another exposed assumption, not a fact about Qwen's program.
:::

Those non-compute lines are substantial:

- data acquisition and governance, and deduplication and filtering at corpus scale;
- storage, transfer between regions, and checkpoint replicas. Under the illustrative recipe of [lesson 5.7](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.7-data-parallelism-zero-fsdp/), a single checkpoint of this model carries about 432 GB of state, so retention policy is a real storage decision;
- evaluation infrastructure, observability, security, and engineering salaries.

Opportunity cost matters too, when scarce cluster capacity delays other work. The BLOOM planning paper framed its model choice explicitly around a one-million-A100-GPU-hour allocation, which is the right way to think about it: the experiment portfolio is planned alongside the target run, not discovered afterwards. Use that figure as evidence about that project at that date, never as a current price.

## How do you account for energy?

Energy follows the same discipline. Device power multiplied by time gives the base: at an illustrative 700 W per accelerator, 430,000 device-hours are about 301 MWh at the chips. A facility multiplier covering hosts, networking, and cooling pushes the energy actually delivered higher. Carbon then depends on where and when that electricity was generated, and multiplying by a generic global intensity factor hides the single largest source of variation. The BLOOM carbon study is useful precisely because it separates the energy the training itself consumed from broader life-cycle boundaries, and says which one it is reporting.

## How should you build and report the estimate?

:::caveat
Public training-cost comparisons are rarely comparable. Some include preliminary experiments and some only final compute; some quote cloud-equivalent rates rather than cash paid; many omit labour and data licensing entirely; and almost none state utilization. Normalize the definitions before comparing two numbers. If you cannot normalize them, say the comparison is not available rather than making one.
:::

Build the estimate as a calculator with exposed variables rather than a hardcoded total. Its inputs are parameters, tokens, an architecture factor, peak throughput, a utilization range, device count, a dated hourly price, a retry fraction, storage, staff, and contingency. Report device-hours, elapsed time at the chosen device count, direct compute cost, a program range, and a sensitivity analysis showing which input moves the answer most. In our example that input is unambiguously $D$, which is also the one that is unknown.

Then reconcile after the run: compare planned and actual tokens, utilization, device-hours, failures, and spend, and feed the differences into the next budget. A cost model that is never checked against reality is marketing arithmetic wearing a spreadsheet.

The chain, end to end, is accounting discipline. FLOPs estimate physical work; utilization maps work to hardware time; price maps time to money; program scope adds everything needed to make the run happen; environmental scope adds energy and emissions. Every headline number is meaningful only with its date, its assumptions, and its boundary attached. For the model this course follows, the most important thing the chain produces is a clear view of exactly which number nobody has published.
