Someone will ask you what it cost to train Qwen3.8-27B. The honest answer has two parts, and both matter: nobody outside the team knows, and here is how you would estimate it if you had the one number that is missing. This lesson builds that estimate end to end, labelling every assumption as it goes, so that what emerges is a method you can apply rather than a figure you can quote.

For a dense decoder, the standard first-order estimate of training work is

$$
F_{train}\approx 6ND,
$$

with $N$ non-embedding parameters and $D$ training tokens. The factor of six approximates forward and backward matrix work — roughly two operations per parameter per token forward, and twice that again backward.

Start with the wrong version, because it is the one people produce. Take the marketed 27 billion parameters, assume 4 trillion tokens, and multiply:

$$
6\times(27\times 10^{9})\times(4\times 10^{12})=6.48\times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs}.
$$

Two things are wrong with treating that as the answer. The smaller error is $N$: lesson 5.3 showed that the 248,320-row embedding and its untied output head together hold about 2.54 billion parameters, and there is a vision tower besides. Non-embedding parameters are closer to 24.5 billion, giving

$$
6\times(2.45\times 10^{10})\times(4\times 10^{12})=5.88\times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs},
$$

about 9% lower. The larger error is $D$. **There is no published token count for this model** — no technical report exists, and the model card documents architecture, licence, sampling presets, and vendor-reported benchmarks only. The 4 trillion above is our assumption, chosen because it is a plausible modern over-trained budget for this parameter count (lesson 5.5), and it is doing more work in this calculation than anything else. Change it to 2 trillion and every number below halves.

A third caveat sits underneath both. The $6ND$ constant was calibrated on uniform attention stacks. Here, 48 of the 64 layers are Gated DeltaNet rather than full attention, which changes the operation count per token and changes how cost grows with sequence length. Treat 5.9e23 as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

Now convert work into time. Realized device-hours are

$$
H=\frac{F_{train}}{P_{peak}\,u\,3600},
$$

where $P_{peak}$ is applicable peak operations per second in the dtype actually used and $u$ is measured utilization. Modern datacenter accelerators deliver bf16 dense throughput on the order of $10^{15}$ FLOP/s — consult the vendor's specification page for a given part rather than trusting a remembered number — and large well-tuned runs commonly land somewhere near 40% model FLOPs utilization. With those two stated assumptions:

$$
H=\frac{5.88\times 10^{23}}{10^{15}\times 0.40\times 3600}=\frac{5.88\times 10^{23}}{1.44\times 10^{18}}\approx 408{,}000\ \text{device-hours}.
$$

On 1,024 accelerators that is $408{,}000/1024\approx 399$ hours, about 17 days of continuous training. At an illustrative rented rate of USD 2.50 per device-hour — a figure that must carry its date, region, and purchasing model, and that ranges by more than a factor of three across on-demand, reserved, and negotiated capacity — the final run costs roughly

$$
408{,}000\times 2.50 = \text{USD } 1{,}020{,}000.
$$

That is one line of the budget, and it is the line everyone quotes as though it were the total.

:::warning
The most common way a training-cost figure misleads is boundary, not arithmetic. "The final run cost about a million" and "the model cost about a million to develop" are different claims, and only the first follows from the chain above. Scaling pilots, data ablations, tokenizer experiments, abandoned architectures, failed runs, rolled-back steps from the incidents of lesson 5.12, evaluation, and post-training are all real device-hours. A program multiplier of 1.5 to 3 times the final run is unremarkable, which puts our illustrative example somewhere between USD 1.5 million and USD 3 million before any non-compute line at all.
:::

Those non-compute lines are substantial: data acquisition and governance, deduplication and filtering at corpus scale, storage and inter-region transfer, checkpoint replicas — and note from lesson 5.7 that a single checkpoint of this model carries about 432 GB of state, so retention policy is a real storage decision — plus evaluation infrastructure, observability, security, and engineering salaries. Opportunity cost matters too when scarce cluster capacity delays other work. The BLOOM planning paper framed model choice explicitly around a one-million-A100-GPU-hour allocation, which is the right way to think about it: the experiment portfolio is planned alongside the target run, not discovered afterwards. Use that figure as evidence about that project at that date, never as a current price.

Energy follows the same discipline. Device power integrated over time gives the base: at an order-of-magnitude 700 W per accelerator, our 408,000 device-hours are about 286 MWh at the chips, and a facility multiplier covering hosts, networking, and cooling — power usage effectiveness, typically well above one — pushes the delivered figure toward 400 MWh. Carbon then depends on where and when that electricity was generated; multiplying by a generic global intensity factor hides the single largest source of variation. The BLOOM carbon study is useful precisely because it separates dynamic training consumption from broader life-cycle boundaries and says which it is reporting.

:::caveat
Public training-cost comparisons are rarely comparable. Some include preliminary experiments and some only final compute; some quote cloud-equivalent rates rather than cash paid; many omit labour and data licensing entirely; and almost none state utilization. Normalize the definitions before comparing two numbers, and if you cannot normalize them, say the comparison is not available rather than making one.
:::

Build the estimate as a calculator with exposed variables rather than a hardcoded total: parameters, tokens, architecture factor, peak throughput, a utilization range, device count, a dated hourly price, retry fraction, storage, staff, and contingency. Report device-hours, elapsed time at the chosen device count, direct compute cost, a program range, and a sensitivity analysis showing which input moves the answer most. In our example that input is unambiguously $D$, which is also the one that is unknown.

Then reconcile after the run — planned versus actual tokens, utilization, device-hours, failures, and spend — and feed the variance into the next budget. A cost model that is never reconciled against reality is marketing arithmetic wearing a spreadsheet.

The durable lesson is accounting discipline. FLOPs estimate physical work; utilization maps work to hardware time; price maps time to money; program scope adds everything needed to make the run happen; environmental scope adds energy and emissions. Every headline number is meaningful only with its date, its assumptions, and its boundary attached — and for the model this course follows, the most important thing that chain produces is a clear view of exactly which number nobody has published.
