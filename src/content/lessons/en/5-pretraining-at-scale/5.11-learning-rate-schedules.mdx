Just before a long pretraining run starts, the cluster allocation grows. Twice as many nodes join, the global batch doubles from four million tokens per step to eight million, throughput improves beautifully, and everyone is pleased. The run finishes on schedule in token terms, and the loss curve looks reasonable. But the final checkpoint is worse than it should be: noticeably worse on downstream evaluation than a smaller pilot predicted. The cause is not the data, the architecture, or the extra nodes. It is that the learning-rate schedule was written in steps.

## What does a learning-rate schedule do?

The optimizer turns gradients into parameter updates, and the **learning rate** sets how large those updates are. One constant rate rarely serves an entire run, because the risk changes over time:

- Early training has uncalibrated activations and unreliable optimizer moments (the running gradient averages Adam keeps, [lesson 2.7](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.7-optimizers/)).
- The middle wants substantial progress.
- The end benefits from small, refining updates.

A **schedule** is a function that sets the learning rate at each point of the run to match that changing risk.

**Warmup** starts below the intended peak and rises over an initial interval. Linear warmup at step $t$ is

$$
\eta_t=\eta_{max}\frac{t}{T_{warmup}}
$$

for $t\le T_{warmup}$. For example, with a peak of 3e-4 and a toy warmup of 2,000 steps, step 500 runs at a quarter of the peak, 7.5e-5. The first updates stay restrained while normalization scales, gradient magnitudes, and Adam's moment estimates settle. Warmup does not repair an excessive peak; it only changes how that peak is approached.

## How does cosine decay behave?

After warmup, **cosine decay** is the modern default. Writing $p$ for the fraction of the decay horizon completed (0 at the start of the decay, 1 at its end),

$$
\eta(p)=\eta_{min}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{max}-\eta_{min})\bigl(1+\cos(\pi p)\bigr).
$$

Evaluate it once by hand with a peak of 3e-4 and a floor of 3e-5, a typical ten-percent minimum. At the quarter mark, $\cos(\pi/4)=0.7071$, so

$$
\eta=\tfrac{1}{2}(2.7\times 10^{-4})(1.7071)+3\times 10^{-5}=2.605\times 10^{-4}.
$$

At the halfway point, $\cos(\pi/2)=0$, so

$$
\eta=\tfrac{1}{2}(2.7\times 10^{-4})(1)+3\times 10^{-5}=1.65\times 10^{-4}.
$$

The curve is gentle near both ends and steepest in the middle. That is why it spends a long time near the peak and then a long time near the floor.

## Why must a schedule be measured in tokens?

Now return to the opening scenario with those numbers in hand. Suppose the plan was a two-trillion-token budget at four million tokens per step, giving 500,000 steps, and the scheduler's horizon was configured as 500,000 steps. Doubling the global batch to eight million tokens means the same two trillion tokens are consumed in 250,000 steps. The run ends when the data ends, at $p=0.5$, with the learning rate still at 1.65e-4. It completed only the first half of the cosine decay and never reached the low final rates. Every refinement that the second half of a cosine provides did not happen, and nothing in the logs looked abnormal, because the rate followed its configured curve faithfully.

:::warning
This is the most common misconception about schedules: that a step is a unit of training progress. It is not. A step is one *call of the optimizer*, and how many tokens it contains depends on world size, microbatch, gradient accumulation, and padding. Define warmup and horizon in tokens, ideally the effective tokens that carry loss, excluding padding. Then the schedule survives topology changes, restarts at a different scale, and the memory and throughput decisions of [lesson 5.10](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.10-gradient-checkpointing/), which change step time without changing data exposure at all.
:::

## What are the alternatives to cosine?

Cosine is not the only shape:

- **Linear decay** falls at a constant pace toward a chosen endpoint, which makes the remaining progress easy to read off.
- **Inverse-square-root** decay, proportional to $t^{-1/2}$ after a warmup construction, was the original Transformer recipe. It has the useful property of not requiring a declared horizon.
- **Constant-with-warmup** keeps the peak after the ramp-up. That suits an uncertain total horizon, but it can leave late updates noisier than a nearly converged model wants.

Endpoints matter as much as shapes. Decaying exactly to zero assumes the run ends where planned: extending the run afterwards produces no learning unless the schedule is rewritten. A nonzero floor keeps the model adapting, but it keeps disturbing a settled model too. Warm restarts, popular elsewhere, deliberately raise the rate again partway through, and they are rarely appropriate for an expensive single-pass pretraining run.

## What else does the schedule interact with?

:::caveat
Learning-rate curves cannot be copied independently of optimizer, batch size, model scale, initialization, precision, clipping, and data mixture. The same numeric peak can be stable in one recipe and destructive in another, which is exactly why the peak rates and warmup lengths of published models are of limited use to you. For Qwen3.8-27B in particular there is no technical report: no peak, warmup, horizon, or token budget is published. What the config does show is the stability machinery the schedule works with, RMSNorm with epsilon 1e-6 in a pre-norm residual stream and gating in both layer types, which [lesson 5.12](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.12-training-instabilities/) takes up.
:::

Three neighbours of the schedule deserve a sentence each:

- **Weight decay.** AdamW separates gradient-based optimization from weight decay (a steady pull of weights toward zero), but the scheduler and the decay still interact. Parameters such as normalization scales and biases are usually excluded from decay entirely.
- **Gradient clipping** caps the size of an update driven by an outsized gradient, before the optimizer step. It is a guardrail, not a substitute for a sane schedule.
- **Batch size.** Scaling the global batch can justify adjusting the peak rate, but linear and square-root scaling rules are heuristics that hold only within limits. Larger batches reduce gradient noise and may want longer warmup or different optimizer tuning, so run pilot sweeps rather than extrapolating a rule across orders of magnitude.

Log the realized rate at every step alongside token count, loss, gradient norm, optimizer statistics, and skipped mixed-precision updates. On resume, restore the scheduler's position. Accidentally restarting warmup after a checkpoint changes the optimization path, and jumping to a wrong late-run rate manufactures exactly the kind of loss spike that lesson 5.12 has to diagnose. Overlaying the rate on the loss curve helps correlate instability with update scale, though correlation alone never proves the schedule caused a spike.

Think of the schedule as controlled momentum for learning. Warmup avoids wrenching an uncalibrated network, the central phase moves quickly through useful parameter space, and decay narrows the step as the run approaches its planned end. The schedule is part of the experiment specification and part of the checkpoint state. It is not cosmetic optimizer configuration, and it should never be expressed in a unit that changes when your cluster does.
