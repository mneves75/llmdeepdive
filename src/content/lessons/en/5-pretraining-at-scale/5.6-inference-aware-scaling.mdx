Training-compute optimality asks how to get the lowest loss out of a fixed pretraining budget. A deployed model lives much longer than that question imagines. Training happens once. Inference happens for every prompt and every generated token, on every replica (each copy of the model serving traffic), for as long as the checkpoint is served. If demand is large, the recurring bill dwarfs the one-time one, and the optimization target has to include both.

## Why does serving demand change the optimum?

A toy example with made-up units shows the effect. Say training costs 100 units once, and serving costs 1 unit per million requests. At 10 million lifetime requests, serving adds 10 units, and training dominates the bill. At a billion requests, serving adds 1,000 units, ten times the training cost. A model that costs twice as much to train but half as much per request now saves money overall.

The standard framing writes lifetime cost as

$$
C_{life}=C_{train}(N,D)+R\,C_{infer}(N,L),
$$

with $R$ the expected inference volume and $L$ capturing prompt and output lengths. Sardana and collaborators modified Chinchilla-style analysis along exactly these lines. They trained models at high token-to-parameter ratios to validate the behaviour, and reported that large enough inference demand shifts the preferred solution toward smaller models trained on more data. Their crossover point depends on their costs, fits, and workload assumptions, but the direction follows from the structure of the formula rather than from those details.

## What can you change besides parameter count?

That is the version of inference-aware scaling everyone quotes, and it treats $N$ as the only lever: pick fewer parameters, feed more tokens, and accept a bigger training bill in exchange for a cheaper forward pass forever. [Lesson 5.5](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.5-chinchilla-compute-optimality/) walked through that arithmetic. It is real, and it is half the story.

The half that gets skipped is that $C_{infer}$ is not a function of parameter count alone. At a fixed 27 billion parameters you can build architectures whose serving costs differ by more than an order of magnitude, because a served request pays for three things:

1. reading the weights,
2. computing the layers,
3. holding the per-request state.

Only the first scales cleanly with $N$. The third, the KV cache (the stored keys and values of earlier tokens that attention reads at every new step), depends entirely on decisions about heads and layer types. That is where Qwen3.8-27B's published architecture makes its serving consequences measurable.

## How does Qwen3.8-27B's layout shrink the cache?

[Lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/) owns the KV-cache arithmetic for this model, so this lesson uses its result instead of re-deriving a second budget. Two published facts matter:

- **Grouped-query attention** (GQA, [lesson 7.6](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.6-mqa-gqa-and-mla/)) lets several query heads share one key–value head. Qwen3.8-27B has 24 query heads but only 4 KV heads.
- The **3:1 hybrid layout** ([lesson 4.16](/lessons/4-transformer/4.16-hybrid-layouts/)) makes three of every four layers Gated DeltaNet, which keeps a fixed-size state instead of a growing cache. Only 16 of the 64 layers are attention layers with a cache.

The relevant counterfactual holds the head dimension and layer count fixed, but replaces the four KV heads with 24 and the 16 caching attention layers with 64. GQA reduces the KV-head count by a factor of six; the 3:1 hybrid layout reduces the number of growing-cache layers by a factor of four. Their structural factors multiply to 24. The exact byte totals, dtype (number format) assumptions, and DeltaNet-state accounting remain in lesson 7.2.

:::insight
Neither change is a lossless configuration switch on the same checkpoint. GQA can, however, be introduced by uptraining (continuing to train) a checkpoint built with full multi-head attention (MHA). The cited GQA paper demonstrates this with about 5% of the original pretraining compute, and the result is a changed checkpoint. Replacing attention layers with DeltaNet likewise requires training the new architecture. These are interventions in model development, unlike a cache optimization that only changes the runtime.
:::

The same distinction applies to the rest of the design. Only 16 layers maintain a cache that grows with sequence length, and the released weights are bf16. Quantized size, usable context, throughput, and whether the model fits on a given accelerator depend on the exact runtime and memory budget. The architecture alone does not establish a workstation target or the team’s intent.

:::warning
The most common misconception here is that inference-aware scaling means "train a smaller model." Parameter count is the lever people know, and it is the coarsest one available. At fixed $N$, the head layout, the layer composition, context handling, and the presence of a native drafter such as multi-token prediction change serving cost by factors that dwarf the few percent of quality those choices cost. If your inference-aware analysis varies only $N$, you are optimizing one axis of a design space with at least four.
:::

## What is known, and what is only inferred?

Honesty about what is and is not known matters here as much as anywhere in this track. The architectural facts above can be verified in the model card and config. The reasoning attributed to them is inference from the design, not a published rationale. There is no technical report for this model, and the team never stated its serving assumptions, token budget, or cost model.

What you can say with confidence is that the shipped architecture reduces the per-sequence state that grows with length, relative to the stated counterfactual. Reading that effect as a team objective is plausible but remains inference.

## What limits an inference-aware analysis?

The usual caveats still apply:

- Forecasts of $R$ are often wrong in both directions, so report how the answer changes across demand scenarios rather than one point estimate.
- Matching validation loss does not guarantee matching quality under latency limits, factuality, multilingual behaviour, or responsiveness to post-training.
- Data availability constrains the over-training lever. Extra unique high-quality tokens may not exist, and repeating data changes the effective objective and raises memorization risk.
- Update cadence matters. A checkpoint replaced next month accumulates far less lifetime inference than one served for years.

The durable principle is to optimize the system's lifetime rather than a single run. Chinchilla asks how to spend a pretraining budget. Inference-aware scaling asks what should exist after training, given how often it will be used. The answer is expressed in tokens, in parameters, and, most consequentially, in the shape of the layers themselves.
