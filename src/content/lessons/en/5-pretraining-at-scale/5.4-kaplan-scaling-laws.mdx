You have budget for exactly one large training run and four candidate configurations. Training all four to find out which was sensible is not an option; the run is the budget. So you do what shipbuilders do with towing tanks: run a family of small, controlled experiments, fit a curve through them, and use the curve to choose. **Scaling laws** are that curve. The remarkable empirical finding behind them is that held-out loss (loss on text the model never trained on) falls along strikingly smooth trends as parameters, data, and compute grow, even though the networks themselves are anything but smooth.

## What shape does the curve have?

The pattern behind the curve is simple to state. Each time you multiply a resource by ten (a **decade**), the part of the loss that can still be removed shrinks by roughly the same fraction. Loss never reaches zero, though: it approaches a floor. A function with that shape is a power law plus a floor:

$$
L(x)=L_\infty + A x^{-\alpha}.
$$

Here $x$ is the non-embedding parameter count, the dataset size, or the training compute. "Non-embedding" means the embedding and output matrices of [lesson 5.3](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.3-training-tokenizer/) are left out of the count. That is a fitting convention: Kaplan and collaborators found that excluding them gives much cleaner trends across model depths. It is not a claim that those matrices cost no compute. The input embedding is a table lookup, but an untied output head still performs a dense logits projection on every token, and [lesson 5.13](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.13-pretraining-costs/) adds that work back. The pieces of the formula:

- $L_\infty$ is the irreducible term: the entropy the data itself carries (its built-in unpredictability, [lesson 1.7](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.7-entropy-perplexity/)), which no model removes.
- $A x^{-\alpha}$ is the reducible part. Plotted with both axes on a log scale (log-log axes), it becomes a straight line whose slope is $-\alpha$.
- A larger positive exponent $\alpha$ means loss falls faster as that resource grows.

## How do you fit the exponent from two runs?

Fit the curve once by hand, because doing so exposes the trap. Suppose two controlled runs share a corpus, tokenizer, and recipe and differ only in size. The run with 100 million non-embedding parameters reaches held-out loss 3.20, and the run with 1 billion reaches 2.80. Pilot work estimates the floor at $L_\infty = 1.70$. Subtract the floor before doing anything else:

$$
1.50 \rightarrow 1.10 \quad\text{across one decade}, \qquad \frac{1.10}{1.50}=0.7333,
$$

$$
\alpha=-\log_{10}(0.7333)=0.135.
$$

So the reducible loss keeps about 73% of its size per tenfold increase in parameters. Now predict a third point. At 10 billion parameters the reducible part should be $1.10\times 0.7333 = 0.807$, so $L \approx 1.70 + 0.81 = 2.51$. You can go and train that run and check the residual, the gap between prediction and measurement. Checking it is the entire point of a fitted law.

## What goes wrong if you skip the floor?

Here is the wrong turn, and it is the one people actually take. Skip the floor and fit the raw losses directly. The ratio is $2.80/3.20 = 0.875$ per decade, giving $\alpha = -\log_{10}(0.875) = 0.058$. Over the two fitted points this looks perfectly respectable: it reproduces both exactly. Now extrapolate four decades, to 10 trillion parameters, and it predicts

$$
2.80\times 0.875^{4}=2.80\times 0.5862=1.64,
$$

a loss **below the irreducible floor of 1.70**. The naive fit has forecast a model that predicts the data better than the data's own entropy allows. Nothing in the arithmetic complains. The impossibility shows up only when you know a floor exists and check against it. Always fit $L_\infty$ jointly with the other terms, and always sanity-check an extrapolation against it.

## What did Kaplan's study find?

Kaplan and collaborators trained many autoregressive Transformer language models and measured loss against model size, data, and compute. Their central structural claim is that performance is limited by whichever resource is too small. Parameters cannot indefinitely make up for insufficient data, and tokens cannot indefinitely make up for a tiny model.

For a fixed compute budget you must therefore choose a point on a tradeoff. A larger model spends more operations per token, so it sees fewer tokens. A smaller one sees more tokens with less capacity. The optimum Kaplan's fits pointed to favoured growing model size relatively fast and stopping training comparatively early. That recommendation shaped early large-model planning until [lesson 5.5](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.5-chinchilla-compute-optimality/)'s Chinchilla result overturned it.

The compute axis usually uses the first-order estimate $C\approx 6ND$ floating-point operations (FLOPs) for $N$ non-embedding parameters and $D$ tokens; the constant depends on what is counted and on the architecture. [Lesson 5.13](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.13-pretraining-costs/) puts it to real use. Note that FLOPs are not wall-clock time: utilization, communication, data loading, recomputation, and failures decide how long a run actually takes.

## Does the curve apply to Qwen3.8-27B?

:::caveat
The exponent and the optimum are empirical estimates, not universal constants. Change the corpus, tokenizer, architecture, optimizer, context length, regularization, or fitting method and the measured surface moves. A scaling law describes the setup it was measured in; it does not dictate what other setups will do.
:::

That caveat matters directly for the model this course follows. Qwen3.8-27B publishes no scaling study, no token count, and no training compute. There is no technical report at all, only the model card and config. So you cannot place it on anyone's fitted curve.

Worse, you should not assume a curve fitted on uniform transformers transfers to it, because its architecture is not uniform. 48 of its 64 layers are Gated DeltaNet rather than full attention ([lesson 4.16](/lessons/4-transformer/4.16-hybrid-layouts/)), which changes the FLOPs-per-token constant and the way cost grows with sequence length. The $6ND$ shorthand and the exponents that accompany it were measured on stacks where every layer was the same. A hybrid stack is a different tank, and the hull curves have to be refitted.

## How do you know when to trust a fitted curve?

Good scaling work separates interpolation (predicting inside the measured range) from extrapolation (predicting beyond it). Fit on some runs, hold others out, and check the prediction error before trusting a curve past the measured range. Plot residuals rather than admiring a straight line: a small systematic deviation becomes expensive across orders of magnitude.

Loss is also not the product. Two models with matching validation loss can differ in downstream tasks, calibration, safety, multilingual coverage, latency, and memory. Benchmark jumps that look like sudden "emergence" often mark a capability that improved smoothly and crossed a scoring threshold.

The $D$ axis carries its own complication, straight out of [lesson 5.2](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.2-data-pipeline/): a token is not a standard unit of information. Duplicated boilerplate, low-quality web text, and curated domain data all count as tokens but contribute differently, so the effective data size depends on duplication and mixture. Hold the pipeline fixed across a scaling study, or model the pipeline change explicitly. Otherwise the exponent you fit is measuring your filters.

The workflow, then, is disciplined rather than clever:

1. Choose several model sizes and token budgets, and keep the recipes comparable.
2. Reserve validation data and log the compute each run actually used.
3. Fit competing functional forms and validate them on withheld runs.
4. Quantify the uncertainty, and refit whenever the data or the architecture changes.

What lasts is predictability with boundaries. The curve makes enormous budget decisions empirical instead of superstitious, and saying where it stops applying is part of the method, not an apology for it.
