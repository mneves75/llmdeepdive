You have a product. It sends Qwen3.8-27B about 200 million input tokens and receives about 40 million output tokens per month: a support assistant with real usage, five parts prompt and context to one part answer. Someone has to decide how it is served, and every argument in that meeting will stand in for a number nobody has computed. This lesson computes it three ways. The method matters more than the winner: state every assumption next to the number it produces, so that when a price or a throughput figure changes, a reader can redo the whole calculation without you.

## Option one: a managed per-token endpoint

A **managed endpoint** is a provider's API that runs the model for you and bills per token. Cloudflare Workers AI lists the model as a first-party offering, and as of August 2026 its published prices are USD 0.45 per million input tokens and USD 3.20 per million output tokens. Multiply each volume, in millions, by its price:

$$
200 \times 0.45 = 90.00, \qquad 40 \times 3.20 = 128.00, \qquad \text{total} = 218.00\ \text{USD per month}.
$$

Note the shape of the bill as much as its size. It is proportional to traffic, so a quiet month costs nearly nothing and a viral week costs proportionally more. There is no capacity to plan, no upgrade path to negotiate, and no servers to operate, which is the argument of [lesson 8.12](/lessons/8-building-and-serving-stacks/8.12-choosing-your-stack/) restated as an invoice.

## Option two: a rented GPU

Now a rented data-center GPU running vLLM, the serving engine. Two assumptions, both stated rather than quoted:

- an 80 GB-class instance at USD 2.50 per hour;
- a sustained total output rate of 800 tokens per second across all users, under continuous batching (the engine adds and removes requests from the running batch at every step).

Neither is a vendor quote; substitute your own from a real provider page and a real benchmark. A month is about 730 hours, and the instance bills for every one of them:

$$
2.50 \times 730\ \text{hours} = 1{,}825.00\ \text{USD per month, independent of traffic}.
$$

Its capacity is the rate times the seconds in a month (3,600 seconds per hour):

$$
800 \times 3600 \times 730 = 2{,}102\ \text{million output tokens of monthly capacity}.
$$

## You rent seconds, not tokens

Here is the usual wrong turn, and it is the reason this lesson exists. The engineer divides rental by capacity and gets

$$
\frac{1825}{2102} \approx 0.87\ \text{USD per million output tokens},
$$

compares it against USD 3.20, announces a 3.7-fold saving, and proposes running the serving stack in-house. The arithmetic is correct and the conclusion is wrong, because you do not rent tokens, you rent seconds. The workload needs only 40 million output tokens, so the instance runs at

$$
\frac{40}{2102} \approx 1.9\%\ \text{utilization}, \qquad \frac{1825}{40} \approx 45.63\ \text{USD per million output tokens},
$$

roughly fourteen times the endpoint price. The saving was real, but it belonged to a workload eight times larger than this one.

Find the crossover explicitly. Treat the workload's mix as a unit: 5 million input tokens plus 1 million output tokens. On the endpoint, one unit costs

$$
5 \times 0.45 + 3.20 = 5.45\ \text{USD}, \qquad \frac{1825}{5.45} \approx 335\ \text{units},
$$

so the rented GPU wins above roughly 1.7 billion input and 335 million output tokens per month. Reassuringly, that is only about 16 percent of the assumed capacity, so the configuration is feasible rather than self-contradictory.

[Lesson 9.3](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.3-kv-cache-sizing-and-memory-budgets/)'s memory budget is what makes that capacity claim checkable at all. 54 GB of weights on an 80 GB card leaves room for a bounded number of concurrent sequences at 64 KiB per cached token. Concurrency is precisely what turns a memory budget into the throughput number in the denominator.

## Option three: owned Apple hardware

Assume a large unified-memory Mac at USD 6,000, **amortized** straight-line over 36 months (its price spread evenly across the months you expect to use it). It draws 150 watts on average across the powered-on month, at USD 0.25 per kilowatt-hour.

$$
\frac{6000}{36} = 166.67, \qquad 150\ \text{W} \times 730\ \text{h} = 109.5\ \text{kWh} \times 0.25 = 27.38,
$$

$$
\text{total} \approx 194\ \text{USD per month}.
$$

[Lesson 9.6](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.6-apple-silicon-unified-memory/) gives only an artifact-size heuristic near 47 tokens per second, which cannot establish capacity. For this scenario, assume a measured sustained 25 output tokens per second. That is about 65.7 million output tokens per month, so the 40-million-token workload keeps the machine busy roughly 61 percent of the time. This holds only if requests do not arrive in bursts that a single-stream machine cannot absorb.

## Lining up the three

Line the three up, and the result is instructive:

| Option | Monthly cost |
| --- | --- |
| Managed endpoint (gross, before the daily allowance) | USD 218 |
| Rented GPU | USD 1,825 |
| Owned Mac | USD 194 |

The managed endpoint and the owned Mac are within twelve percent of each other, a rounding error next to how much they differ in everything else. The rented GPU loses by an order of magnitude purely on utilization.

:::warning
Cash is not the comparison. The three options are not serving the same artifact: the owned Mac runs a 4-bit quantized build whose quality differs from whatever precision the endpoint serves, so [lesson 7.9](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.9-quantization-i-int8-int4-the-basics/)'s accuracy question is hiding inside a spreadsheet cell. The Mac has no redundancy, no autoscaling, one stream, and an operator (you) whose time appears on no line. The endpoint sends your users' text to a third party, which is a compliance question no cost model answers. And the vendor-reported benchmark scores on the model card describe the model, not your deployment of it.
:::

:::caveat
Every figure here is either a date-stamped published price or an assumption written down so you can replace it. Prices as of August 2026 change without notice; the USD 2.50 hourly rate, the 800 tokens per second, the 150 watts, the USD 0.25 per kilowatt-hour and the 36-month amortization are all mine to assume and yours to override. Redo the three divisions with your own numbers before quoting any of these results. Re-run them whenever a price page changes, your traffic shape shifts, a newer quantization changes the artifact size, or a cheaper accelerator generation lands.
:::

## The formula behind every option

The general form is worth memorizing, because it survives every price change: cost per token is system cost per unit time divided by tokens produced per unit time. Every lesson in this track fed one of those two terms:

- bandwidth and the roofline set the achievable rate;
- the KV-cache budget sets concurrency, which sets the total rate;
- quantization moves both;
- the interconnect decides whether extra chips actually add throughput;
- utilization, the term nobody puts on a slide, decides whether fixed costs are a bargain or a liability.

That closes this track. You can now size a deployment you have never seen: read a config file, derive its memory and bandwidth demands, choose a stack, pick hardware, and defend the choice with arithmetic instead of vendor slides. For this model, the facts that drive every line are a distribution over 248,320 tokens per step, 16 of 64 layers caching 64 KiB per token ([lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/)), and the other 48 holding a flat 144 MiB in the reference float32 path (lesson 9.3).
