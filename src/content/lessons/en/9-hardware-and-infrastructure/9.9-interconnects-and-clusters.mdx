[Lesson 9.4](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.4-nvidia-cuda-hopper-blackwell/) left you with a sizing problem. Qwen3.8-27B in bf16 is 54 GB of weights, and [lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/) adds up to 16 GiB of KV cache for one full-length sequence. A single 80 GB accelerator therefore serves the model with very little room for users. The obvious fix is more GPUs. This lesson answers what that actually costs, because the wire between the chips has a price that appears on no spec sheet you were shopping from.

## What has to cross the wire for each token?

[Lesson 5.8](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.8-model-parallelism/) established the mechanism. Under **tensor parallelism**, each weight matrix is split across **ranks** (the GPUs taking part, numbered 0 to N−1). Attention and DeltaNet heads are distributed one way, the feed-forward network's up and down projections another. Every rank computes a partial result over its slice, and the slices must be summed before the residual stream (the vector each token carries from layer to layer) continues.

In the standard arrangement, each layer has two such reconciliation points:

1. after the output projection that follows the sequence mixer (the attention or DeltaNet part of the layer);
2. after the feed-forward down projection.

Each reconciliation is an **all-reduce**: every rank contributes its partial vector and every rank ends up with the sum. For the token being decoded, it covers a single hidden vector.

That is the whole worked example, and it is small enough to do in your head. Hidden size 5120, bf16 (2 bytes per number):

$$
5120 \times 2\ \text{B} = 10{,}240\ \text{B} = 10\ \text{KiB per reconciliation}.
$$

$$
64\ \text{layers} \times 2\ \text{reconciliations} = 128\ \text{all-reduces per token},
$$

$$
128 \times 10\ \text{KiB} = 1{,}280\ \text{KiB} = 1.25\ \text{MiB of payload per token}.
$$

A ring all-reduce, the common algorithm in which ranks pass pieces around a ring, puts $\tfrac{2(N-1)}{N}$ times the payload on each rank's wire. So two GPUs move 1.25 MiB per token and four move about 1.875 MiB. At an interactive 50 tokens per second, that is under 100 MiB per second per rank. NVLink, NVIDIA's direct GPU-to-GPU link, is vendor-reported at hundreds of gigabytes per second, so the traffic is a rounding error there, and comfortable even for PCIe, the ordinary expansion bus.

## The bytes are not the problem; the round trips are

This is exactly where the usual wrong turn happens. An engineer computes that payload, sees it is tiny, concludes the interconnect is irrelevant, and buys four GPUs on a PCIe-only board expecting four times the throughput of one.

The error is measuring the wrong quantity. The 128 all-reduces per token are not one transfer of 1.25 MiB. They are 128 blocking round trips, one after another, and for a small collective (a communication step involving every rank) the cost is dominated by latency, not bandwidth. Think of 128 sequential small HTTP requests: their total time is set by round-trip time, not by how many bytes each one carries.

Put stated assumptions on the latency (assumptions to make the shape visible, not vendor figures) and redo the arithmetic. Say a fast fabric inside one machine costs about 10 microseconds per small all-reduce and a slow one about 50. For a stated checkpoint-size scenario, apply [lesson 9.1](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.1-memory-bandwidth-vs-flops/)'s division to each GPU's share of the file; this is a planning heuristic, not measured active traffic:

$$
N = 2:\quad \frac{27\ \text{GB}}{3.35\ \text{TB/s}} \approx 8.06\ \text{ms}, \qquad N = 4:\quad \frac{13.5\ \text{GB}}{3.35\ \text{TB/s}} \approx 4.03\ \text{ms}.
$$

Communication adds a fixed 128 times the per-collective latency: 1.28 ms on the fast fabric, 6.4 ms on the slow one. Adding those stated heuristics and latency assumptions:

$$
N = 2\ \text{fast}: 8.06 + 1.28 = 9.34\ \text{ms} \rightarrow 107\ \text{tok/s},
$$

$$
N = 4\ \text{fast}: 4.03 + 1.28 = 5.31\ \text{ms} \rightarrow 188\ \text{tok/s},
$$

$$
N = 4\ \text{slow}: 4.03 + 6.40 = 10.43\ \text{ms} \rightarrow 96\ \text{tok/s}.
$$

## Reading the three lines

Read those three lines carefully.

- Doubling from two to four GPUs on the fast fabric returned 1.76 times, not 2. The checkpoint-size term halved while the assumed communication stayed fixed.
- Four GPUs on the slow fabric are slower than two on the fast one.

The interconnect, not the accelerator count, decided the outcome. That is why NVLink exists as a product distinct from PCIe, and why tensor parallelism is kept inside a node (one machine). Cross-node fabrics such as InfiniBand or RoCE (networks that link machines) are reserved for the forms of parallelism that reconcile rarely:

- **data parallelism**, which synchronizes once per training step;
- **pipeline parallelism** over the model's 16 super-blocks (repeating groups of layers), which passes an activation from one stage to the next instead of reconciling every layer.

## Prefill is the opposite case

Prefill behaves the other way, and it is worth seeing why. A 4,096-token prompt runs all positions at once, so the same 128 collectives each carry 4,096 hidden vectors:

$$
4096 \times 1.25\ \text{MiB} \approx 5\ \text{GiB of all-reduce payload for one prefill}.
$$

Now the bytes genuinely matter and bandwidth governs. This is the arithmetic-intensity split of [lesson 7.3](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.3-prefill-vs-decode-two-different-machines/) reappearing on the network instead of on memory. Prefill is bandwidth-bound over the fabric; decode is latency-bound over it.

:::warning
A frequent misconception is that the tensor-parallel degree (the number of GPUs a layer is split across) is a tuning knob you can set freely. The model's own shapes constrain it. Qwen3.8-27B has 24 query heads and 4 KV heads. The FFN intermediate size is 17,408 and the padded vocabulary is 248,320, and both divide cleanly by 4, 8 and well beyond; the vocabulary padding from [lesson 5.3](/lessons/5-pretraining-at-scale/5.3-training-tokenizer/) exists precisely to make that true. The shape that binds first is the 4 KV heads. At degree 4, each rank owns exactly one KV head and the cache shards perfectly. At degree 8, the KV heads must be replicated across pairs of ranks, so each rank stores a duplicate of the same cache entries. The KV-cache saving then stops growing, although the weights and the FFN still shard, just as the communication fraction is climbing. Degree 8 is a cost to price, not an impossibility. The grouped-query design of [lesson 7.6](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.6-mqa-gqa-and-mla/) that made the cache small is the same design that limits how far the cache shards.
:::

:::caveat
The latency figures above are assumptions stated so you can substitute your own, and the 3.35 TB/s and NVLink bandwidths are vendor-published specifications, not measurements taken here. The 128-collective count is a first-order model of a standard tensor-parallel layout: real implementations fuse operations, overlap communication with computation, and may reconcile differently in the Gated DeltaNet layers. Profile your own configuration with the collective library's instrumentation before committing to a topology, and never quote this arithmetic as a benchmark.
:::

The rule for cluster design is to put the parallelism that reconciles most often on the fastest wire you own, and to split a model where its own dimensions divide cleanly unless you have priced the replication. For this model, that usually means tensor parallelism inside one node, at a degree of 2 or 4, on NVLink, and everything else across the network. Degree 8 works too, but it pays for duplicated KV and more synchronization, so measure it before choosing it. [Lesson 9.10](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.10-cost-per-token/) takes those configurations and finally prices them.
