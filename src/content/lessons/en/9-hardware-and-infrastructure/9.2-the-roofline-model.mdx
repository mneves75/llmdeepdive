A profiling report lands on your desk. The decode kernel for Qwen3.8-27B achieves three tenths of one percent of the GPU's peak floating-point rate. The obvious reading is that somebody wrote a terrible kernel and a rewrite will recover two orders of magnitude. The roofline model exists to tell you, in about thirty seconds and without touching the code, that this reading is almost certainly wrong and that the kernel may already be perfect.

## Two numbers for the machine, one for the kernel

Williams, Waterman, and Patterson published the model in 2009 to make exactly this judgement cheap. It compresses a machine into two numbers and a kernel (one function the GPU runs) into one:

- The machine contributes its **peak arithmetic rate**, in operations per second, and its **peak memory bandwidth**, in bytes per second.
- The kernel contributes its **arithmetic intensity**: the operations it performs per byte it must move from memory, the ratio [lesson 9.1](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.1-memory-bandwidth-vs-flops/) introduced.

Take a toy kernel that does 2 operations per byte on a made-up machine with a bandwidth of 10 bytes per second and a peak of 100 operations per second. Memory can feed it 10 bytes each second, and each byte carries 2 operations, so memory allows 20 operations per second. The arithmetic units could do 100. The kernel gets the smaller number, 20, and the memory is its limit. Raise the intensity to 50 operations per byte and memory would allow 500, so now the arithmetic ceiling of 100 is the limit.

That is the whole rule. Attainable performance is the smaller of what the arithmetic units can deliver and what the memory system can feed:

$$
P_{\text{attainable}}(I) \ =\ \min\left(P_{\text{peak}},\ \beta \cdot I\right),
$$

where $\beta$ is bandwidth in bytes per second and $I$ is intensity in operations per byte.

## Reading the chart

Plot intensity on the horizontal axis and performance on the vertical axis, both on logarithmic scales (each gridline is ten times the previous one). The formula then draws a slanted line of slope one (doubling intensity doubles $\beta \cdot I$) and stops abruptly at a flat ceiling. The bend is the **ridge point**, the intensity at which the two limits are equal:

$$
I^{*} \ =\ \frac{P_{\text{peak}}}{\beta}.
$$

Using the vendor-reported H100-class figures from lesson 9.1, roughly one thousand teraflops of dense bf16 against 3.35 TB/s of HBM3 (the GPU's high-bandwidth memory), the ridge point lands near 300 operations per byte. Those figures are as of Aug 2026 and carry every hedge lesson 9.1 attached to them.

Left of the ridge, the memory system is the constraint, and a kernel there is called **bandwidth-bound**. Right of it, arithmetic is the constraint, and the kernel is **compute-bound**. That is the entire model, and it is enough to price most optimization proposals before anyone writes code.

## Prefill sits on the flat roof

Place Qwen3.8-27B on the chart. **Prefill**, the phase that reads the whole prompt at once, carries the intensity established in [lesson 7.3](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.3-prefill-vs-decode-two-different-machines/). For an 8,192-token prompt that is about 8,192 operations per weight byte, because each weight fetched serves all prompt positions simultaneously. That is roughly 27 times past the ridge point, so prefill rides the flat compute roof and its attainable performance is $P_{\text{peak}}$.

The honest engineering questions there are about tensor-core utilization (how fully the tensor cores are kept busy), tiling (splitting a matrix product into blocks that fit in fast on-chip memory), and number format. Those are what move you along a ceiling you are actually pressed against. Time to first token, how long the user waits before the answer starts, responds to arithmetic. Buying FLOPs for prefill is a defensible purchase.

## Decode sits on the slanted roof

Batch-1 decode, generating one token for one sequence per step, carries an intensity near one. Its attainable performance is therefore

$$
P_{\text{attainable}} \ =\ 3.35\times10^{12}\ \frac{\text{B}}{\text{s}} \times 1\ \frac{\text{op}}{\text{B}} \ =\ 3.35\ \text{TFLOP/s},
$$

which against a thousand-teraflop ceiling is about 0.34 percent of peak. So the profiling report describes a kernel sitting exactly on its roofline. It is not inefficient; it is under a different roof. Nothing in the instruction stream can be reordered to fix it, because the arithmetic units were never the scarce resource. Knowing this saves teams from months of kernel micro-optimization that recovers single-digit percentages.

What does move the point is the workload. Batching 32 sequences reads each weight once for 32 tokens, which moves the point to intensity 32 and allows about 107 teraflops. That is roughly a thirty-twofold gain, obtained entirely by changing the workload rather than the kernel. It is still an order of magnitude short of the ridge, so batched decode is still on the slanted roof.

## One model, two kinds of layer

The hybrid layout puts two distinct dots on the chart for one model, which is what makes Qwen3.8-27B interesting to profile.

The 16 full-attention layers read a key-value history (the KV cache of [lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/)) that grows at 64 KiB per token, the figure derived in that lesson. The arithmetic each new query performs against that history grows at the same rate. So their intensity stays roughly constant and low, but the absolute bytes per step climb. As context lengthens, these layers take a rising share of the step time on the bandwidth roof.

The 48 Gated DeltaNet layers behave differently. They read and update a fixed recurrent state, a summary of the past with a fixed size, of roughly 3 MiB per layer: about 144 MiB in total in the reference float32 path, no matter how long the conversation has run. Their dot does not move. Three quarters of the model's depth therefore adds a constant, bounded cost to every decode step, while a quarter adds a growing one. That is the architecture's trade-off restated in roofline terms.

:::warning
The most common misuse of the roofline is treating arithmetic intensity as a property of the algorithm alone. It is a property of the algorithm as executed on a specific memory hierarchy, the chain of memories from slow and large (HBM) to fast and small (L2 cache, on-chip shared memory). Fusing two kernels so an intermediate result never reaches HBM raises intensity without changing the mathematics. Tiling attention so the score matrix stays in on-chip memory does the same, which is precisely what the IO-aware attention of [lesson 7.7](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.7-flashattention-and-io-awareness/) exploits. Two implementations of identical math routinely sit on opposite sides of the ridge point. Always ask which level of the hierarchy the roofline is drawn against, because a chart against HBM and a chart against L2 tell different stories about the same kernel.
:::

## From sketch to evidence

Turning this sketch into evidence requires real counters, not estimates. Kernel profilers report achieved bandwidth and achieved arithmetic separately. They can place a measured kernel on a measured roofline, including secondary roofs for L2 and shared memory. That matters because the intensity you derive on paper and the intensity a kernel actually shows diverge whenever caching, padding, or memory layout differ from the idealization.

:::caveat
The roofline is deliberately crude. It assumes computation and data movement overlap perfectly, ignores latency and occupancy effects, and treats peak numbers that no kernel attains as reachable. It also says nothing about kernel launch overhead, which genuinely matters when decode steps are only milliseconds long. Use it to decide which resource to attack and to reject proposals that attack the wrong one. Do not use it to predict a throughput figure you intend to promise anyone.
:::

The payoff is a habit. Before optimizing, estimate intensity, compare it to the ridge point, and let the chart tell you whether you are shopping for bytes or for operations. [Lesson 9.4](/lessons/9-hardware-and-infrastructure/9.4-nvidia-cuda-hopper-blackwell/) applies that habit to specific silicon, where a newer part changes both roofs at once and the capacity underneath them.
