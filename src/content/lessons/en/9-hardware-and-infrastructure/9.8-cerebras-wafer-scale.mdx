Lesson 9.1 ended on an uncomfortable number. Generating one token at batch one requires reading essentially every resident weight byte, so the rate is capped by

$$
\text{tokens per second} \le \frac{B}{W},
$$

with $B$ the memory bandwidth and $W$ the bytes of weights. For Qwen3.8-27B in bf16, $W$ is 54 GB, and an H100 SXM's reported 3.35 TB/s of HBM3 gives roughly 60 tokens per second as a hard ceiling. Every subsequent lesson in this track has accepted that structure and argued about the numerator or the denominator. This lesson is about a design that attacks the denominator's physics rather than its value.

The reason HBM bandwidth is where it is comes down to where the memory sits. High-bandwidth memory is DRAM stacked beside the processor die and reached through an interposer — vastly better than a socketed DIMM, but still an off-die interface with a finite number of wires running at a finite rate. SRAM, the memory used for on-chip caches, sits in the same silicon as the arithmetic and is reached without leaving the die. Its bandwidth is orders of magnitude higher, and its capacity per unit of silicon area is orders of magnitude lower. That single trade is the whole story of every cache hierarchy ever built: a small fast tier in front of a large slow one, with a hit rate deciding which one you actually feel.

Wafer-scale processing takes the trade to its logical extreme. Instead of cutting a wafer into hundreds of separate chips, the entire wafer is fabricated as one device, tiled with a very large number of small cores each carrying its own local SRAM, connected by an on-wafer fabric. Cerebras describes this architecture and the inference service built on it in its product and documentation pages. The consequence for language-model decode is direct: if the model's weights are distributed across that on-wafer SRAM, the per-token weight read never touches an HBM interface, and the denominator of lesson 9.1's inequality is replaced by something much larger.

How much larger? Rather than quoting a bandwidth figure and asking you to trust it, invert the formula and turn a public throughput claim into a bandwidth requirement. Vendors in this space advertise rates above a thousand tokens per second on large models. Take that claim at bf16 for our specimen:

$$
1000\ \text{tok/s} \times 54\ \text{GB per token} = 54\ \text{TB/s of effective weight-read bandwidth}.
$$

Fifty-four terabytes per second — about sixteen times an H100's HBM3 figure. At the 4-bit artifact size of roughly 16 GB from lesson 7.12 the requirement softens but stays severe:

$$
1000\ \text{tok/s} \times 16\ \text{GB per token} = 16\ \text{TB/s}.
$$

Still nearly five times HBM3. This is the useful discipline: a throughput claim implies a bandwidth, and you can check whether the implied bandwidth is plausible for the memory technology in question. From stacked DRAM these rates are not merely difficult, they are outside the interface's physics. From on-wafer SRAM they are dimensionally consistent. That does not make them true — it makes them the kind of claim worth investigating rather than dismissing.

Now the wrong turn, which is the reason this lesson sits before the cost capstone rather than after it. A reader computes that a wafer-scale system is roughly sixteen times faster per stream than a GPU and concludes it must therefore be roughly sixteen times cheaper per token. Two errors are stacked there. The first is treating a roofline bound as a throughput prediction: lesson 9.1's inequality is an upper bound only, and achieved rates depend on kernel efficiency, compiler quality, the fabric's behaviour under the model's actual dataflow, and whether every operator — including this model's Gated DeltaNet layers — maps onto the hardware at all. The second error is comparing the wrong quantity. Nobody serves a production GPU at batch one. Lesson 7.3 showed that batching amortizes the weight read across many concurrent sequences, so a GPU's aggregate throughput climbs far above its single-stream ceiling while each user still sees roughly single-stream latency. The GPU's product is throughput per dollar; the wafer's product is latency per stream. Those are different goods, and lesson 9.10 will show that cost per token is set by system cost per second divided by tokens per second, never by a speed ratio alone.

:::warning
The most common misconception here is that wafer scale repeals the memory wall. It relocates it. On-wafer SRAM capacity is small relative to DRAM for the same silicon, so a model whose weights exceed what one wafer holds must be split across several or streamed from a separate weight store — and the moment weights stream from off-wafer memory, an interface is back in the path and lesson 9.1's arithmetic applies to that interface. Read a vendor architecture description for where the weights actually reside during decode before assuming the SRAM figure governs your workload. The KV cache is a separate question again: lesson 7.2's 16 GiB at full native context does not shrink because the compute substrate changed.
:::

:::caveat
Every performance number attributed to a wafer-scale system in this lesson is vendor-reported and, as of August 2026, awaiting broad independent reproduction under disclosed conditions. When you read such a figure, ask four things: which model and which precision, what context length, what batch size, and whether the number is peak or sustained. A thousand tokens per second at 4-bit and 2,000 tokens of context is a different claim from the same rate at bf16 and 262,144 tokens, and only the first is comparable to the numbers this course has derived.
:::

The durable takeaway is a method, not a verdict on a vendor. The bandwidth wall of lesson 9.1 is not a law about GPUs, it is a law about the ratio of supply rate to bytes moved — and any architecture that changes where the weights physically sit changes that ratio. Wafer scale is one such architecture; quantization is another, attacking the numerator instead; the hybrid Gated DeltaNet layout of lesson 4.16 is a third, reducing what must be read at all. Cost decides which one you can afford, and that is the last lesson of the course.
