[Lesson 4.2](/lessons/4-transformer/4.2-self-attention/) left one part of the attention formula unexplained: the division by $\sqrt{d_k}$. As a reminder, each token produces a **query** (what it is looking for) and a **key** (what it offers), and the dot product of one query with one key is that pair's raw attention score. $d_k$ is the length of those vectors, the head dimension. This lesson answers two questions: why the scores must be divided by $\sqrt{d_k}$, and how many scores there are at full context.

## Why does a wider head produce bigger scores?

The problem appears the moment the head dimension changes. Compare two hypothetical heads scoring the same token pair, one 4-dimensional and one 256-dimensional. Even if each individual feature carries the same amount of signal, the wide head's dot product adds up 64 times more terms. So its raw scores swing over a far larger range, and something downstream is about to be very sensitive to that range.

Write one score as

$$
s = q \cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i.
$$

To measure how far such a sum typically swings, statistics uses the **variance** (the average squared distance from the mean) and its square root, the **standard deviation** (a typical size of the swing). The original Transformer paper reasons under a simple assumption: the components are roughly independent, with mean zero and variance one. Then each product $q_i k_i$ has variance near one. Variances of independent terms add, so the sum has $\operatorname{Var}(s) \approx d_k$ and standard deviation near $\sqrt{d_k}$. For Qwen3.8-27B's attention heads, $d_k = 256$: typical raw scores are on the order of $\sqrt{256} = 16$ times larger than a single feature's contribution, purely because of width.

## What do large scores do to softmax?

Softmax exponentiates its inputs, so the scale of the scores changes its behavior. The first approach anyone would try is to feed raw scores straight into softmax. That works acceptably for tiny heads and goes wrong for wide ones without raising any error.

Walk through it with three keys. Suppose the "true" preference pattern is mild: one key twice as compatible as the next. At small scale the raw scores might be $[0.5, 0.25, 0]$, and softmax spreads meaningful weight across all three. Now let the 256-wide head inflate everything by its natural factor of 16: the same pattern becomes $[8, 4, 0]$. Softmax of $[8, 4, 0]$ puts roughly 98% of the weight on the first key. That is near winner-take-all certainty the model never earned.

This state has a name: softmax is **saturated** when its outputs are pushed close to zero and one. In the saturated region most gradients are nearly zero, so learning stalls exactly where the model should be adjusting.

## The fix: divide by the square root of the head size

The correction is to divide by the standard deviation that the width introduced:

$$
\operatorname{Attention}(Q,K,V)
= \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V.
$$

With Qwen's numbers, the inflated scores $[8, 4, 0]$ divided by $\sqrt{256} = 16$ come back to $[0.5, 0.25, 0]$, the range where softmax still spreads weight and gradients still flow.

The divisor can also be read as a fixed softmax **temperature** of $T = \sqrt{d_k} = 16$: a scale chosen once from geometry rather than exposed as a knob. The model can still learn sharper or flatter attention by growing or shrinking the lengths (norms) of its queries and keys. The scaling only removes the automatic sharpening that width alone would cause.

:::caveat
Scaled attention weights are not calibrated probabilities about truth or importance. They are normalized routing weights inside one head at one layer, shaped by learned representations. The √d_k keeps them trainable; it does not make them meaningful as confidences. And the tidy variance story holds at initialization. Trained networks violate the independence assumptions, which is fine, because the divisor's job was to remove the *systematic* growth with dimension, not to enforce exact unit variance forever.
:::

## Scaling and max-subtraction fix different problems

Numerical stability is a separate fix that works alongside the scaling. Practical softmax subtracts the row maximum before exponentiating. [Lesson 0.3](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.3-probability-you-need/) showed that adding the same constant to every input leaves softmax unchanged, so this prevents overflow without changing the resulting distribution. Max-subtraction solves *representability*, keeping the numbers inside what floating point can store; $\sqrt{d_k}$ solves *saturation*. You need both.

## How big is the score matrix at full context?

The second reason this lesson matters is the size of the thing being normalized. The score matrix has one entry per query–key pair. Qwen3.8-27B's native context length is 262,144 tokens. At full context, one head of one layer scores

$$
262{,}144 \times 262{,}144 = 2^{18} \times 2^{18} = 2^{36} \approx 6.9 \times 10^{10}
$$

query–key pairs: about 69 billion score entries. That is before multiplying by the model's 24 query heads, and before noting that 16 of its 64 layers run this computation. Nothing about the √256 division changes that count. Scaling fixes the *statistics* of the scores, not their *quantity*.

This is the quadratic bill from [lesson 4.1](/lessons/4-transformer/4.1-attention-is-all-you-need-in-context/) made concrete, and it is the standing motivation for track 7. Three answers wait there: FlashAttention-style kernels that avoid storing the whole matrix in memory at once; the KV cache, whose arithmetic lands in [lesson 7.2](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.2-the-kv-cache/); and the grouped-query design from [lesson 7.6](/lessons/7-inference-and-efficiency/7.6-mqa-gqa-and-mla/), which Qwen already bakes into its 24-query/4-KV head layout. Qwen's deeper answer, replacing attention entirely in 48 of 64 layers, arrives in [lesson 4.15](/lessons/4-transformer/4.15-gated-deltanet/).

The idea reaches beyond this one denominator. Whenever a sum collects many roughly independent contributions, its typical size grows with the number of terms. Passing it into a sensitive nonlinearity then changes behavior merely because width changed. The Transformer compensates with a scale derived from the variance: a two-character fix in the formula that matters at every width, from a toy head to 256.
