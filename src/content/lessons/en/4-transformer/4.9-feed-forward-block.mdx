## Where do 27 billion parameters sit?

Start with an accounting question. Qwen3.8-27B is marketed at roughly 27 billion parameters, so where do they physically sit? Attention earns the publicity because it draws visible links between tokens. But if you open the checkpoint and add up tensor sizes, the answer is unglamorous: around 17 billion parameters, the clear majority of the model, live in the **feed-forward network** (FFN) repeated in every one of the 64 layers. Attention routes information; the FFN transforms it; and the FFN is where most of the learned capacity resides.

## The original two-matrix block

The original Transformer block applies two linear maps with a nonlinearity between them (the multilayer perceptron of [lesson 2.2](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.2-multilayer-perceptrons-nonlinearity/)):

$$
\operatorname{FFN}(x)=W_2\,\sigma(W_1x+b_1)+b_2.
$$

Here $x$ is one token's vector, $W_1$ and $W_2$ are weight matrices, $b_1$ and $b_2$ are bias vectors, and $\sigma$ stands for a nonlinear activation function ([lesson 2.3](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.3-activation-functions/); the 2017 paper used ReLU). The first projection expands from model width $d_{model}$ to a larger intermediate width $d_{ff}$. The second projects back so the result can rejoin the residual stream (the running per-token vector, [lesson 4.10](/lessons/4-transformer/4.10-residuals-pre-norm-post-norm/)).

Three properties matter. The same weights apply at every sequence position. Positions are processed in parallel. And no information moves between tokens inside the block.

## Qwen's gated version, on two numbers

Qwen3.8-27B uses the gated descendant of this idea. Its FFN forms three projections instead of two:

$$
\operatorname{FFN}(x)=W_{down}\left(\operatorname{SiLU}(W_{gate}\,x)\odot W_{up}\,x\right).
$$

$W_{gate}$ and $W_{up}$ both expand the 5120-dimensional hidden state to an intermediate width of 17,408. The gate path passes through SiLU, the smooth activation $z\,\sigma(z)$, where this time $\sigma$ means specifically the logistic sigmoid (a curve from 0 to 1). The result multiplies the up path element-wise; that is what $\odot$ means. One projection proposes candidate features; the other decides, feature by feature, how strongly each one fires. $W_{down}$ then compresses the 17,408 activations back to 5120 for the residual addition.

A toy case with two intermediate features shows the gate at work. Suppose the up path proposes the value 3 for both features. The gate path computes 2 for the first and −2 for the second. SiLU turns 2 into about 1.76 and −2 into about −0.24. After the element-wise product, the first feature is about 5.3 and the second about −0.7: one passed strongly, the other was nearly shut off.

## Counting the parameters

Now count parameters, and count them the way most people first get it wrong. The instinct trained on the two-matrix formula says: two matrices of $5120\times 17408\approx 89.1$ million entries each, so about 178 million parameters per layer. That misses the gate.

The gated block holds three matrices: gate and up at $5120\times 17408$, down at $17408\times 5120$. Each is around 89.1 million parameters, giving approximately 267 million per layer. Multiply by 64 layers and the FFNs alone account for roughly 17 billion parameters of the ~27 billion total. The attention-side projections, by comparison, are a small fraction of a layer's budget.

Each FFN matrix also costs about 89.1 million multiply-adds per token (one multiplication and one addition per weight). So this block dominates compute as well as storage for typical context lengths.

## What the FFN learns, and what it cannot see

Why spend the budget on width? A wide intermediate space is a large bank of pattern detectors:

- a row of $W_{gate}$ or $W_{up}$ responds strongly when the incoming residual vector aligns with its learned direction;
- the gate suppresses or admits that response;
- columns of $W_{down}$ then write combinations of surviving features back into the 5120-dimensional model space.

The block behaves less like a generic "extra layer" and more like a learned feature dictionary consulted at every position.

Research has sharpened that intuition into the key–value memory lens: input directions act as keys that recognize patterns, output directions as values that write associated information. It is a productive picture. A hidden activation can respond to a pattern like "capital of France is" assembled by attention at the token "Paris" and add a feature that the output head associates with a likely continuation. It also helps explain why targeted FFN edits can change factual behavior.

:::caveat
"Facts live in the FFN" is too literal. Behavior emerges from the whole computation: embeddings establish features, the mixing layers retrieve context, residual connections superpose updates, dozens of FFNs compose across depth, and the output head reads the final state. A factual association may depend on a route through many components, and individual neurons are not database rows with stable natural-language labels.
:::

Notice what the FFN never does: mix positions or apply any positional operation. The block applies identical weights at token 3 and token 200,000, and it performs no operation across positions. Everything positional you studied in the last lessons (RoPE rotating queries and keys in [lesson 4.7](/lessons/4-transformer/4.7-rope/), ALiBi adding distance-dependent penalties to attention scores in [lesson 4.8](/lessons/4-transformer/4.8-alibi-relative-bias/)) acts inside the mixing sublayer, never here. If the FFN at one position behaves differently from another, that is because the residual content it receives differs, and that content can encode position that earlier mixing layers wrote into it; the FFN itself is never told where it is.

:::insight
The mixer communicates but, once its routing weights are fixed, acts largely linearly on values. The FFN supplies the strong position-wise nonlinearity that turns a retrieved combination of signals into a genuinely new feature rather than a forwarded copy. Alternating the two (communicate, then compute) is the core rhythm of the architecture.
:::

## Dense, not mixture-of-experts

Later architectural choices reshape this block's economics rather than its role. Sparse mixture-of-experts models replace one dense FFN with a router and several expert FFNs, activating a subset per token. Qwen3.8-27B does not do this: its FFNs are dense, and every token pays the full 267 million parameters per layer.

When you inspect a real FFN, track both shapes and residual effects: which directions activate, what they write, how large the update is against the existing stream, and whether later layers preserve it. Activation alone is not causation. Ablation (switching a component off and measuring what changes) and controlled intervention give stronger evidence.

The stable mental model is a factory rhythm. Attention is the courier bringing each address its selected notes. The FFN is the machine shop: it expands the local state across 17,408 workbenches, gates each bench with SiLU, and compresses an update back onto the shared conveyor. Sixty-four shops in a row hold seventeen billion of the twenty-seven billion parts: this is where most of the model lives.
