## What DPO removes from the pipeline

[Lesson 6.5](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.5-rlhf-with-ppo/) left you holding four models at once and a 27B-parameter policy whose optimizer state alone runs to hundreds of gigabytes. Most teams cannot run that, and many do not need to.

**Direct Preference Optimization** (DPO) starts from a mathematical relationship between the best possible policy under a KL penalty and the reward it was optimized for. It then rearranges that relationship so the policy can be trained directly on preference pairs. There is no reward model, no rollout workers sampling new responses, and no value head. What survives is a forward pass and a loss.

## What DPO compares for each pair

DPO needs one number per response: its **log-probability**, the sum of the logs of the probabilities the model gives to each of the response's tokens, each given the prompt and the response tokens before it. It is negative, and closer to zero means the model finds the whole response more likely.

DPO computes that number twice for each response. Once under the **policy** being trained, and once under a frozen **reference**, usually the starting checkpoint. The difference says how much more the policy now favours the response than the reference already did. DPO wants that gain to be larger for the chosen response than for the rejected one. The gap between the two gains, scaled by a setting $\beta$, is a margin, and the loss is the same logistic loss as the reward model's in [lesson 6.4](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.4-reward-models-and-human-preference-data/).

Run one example through the arithmetic. Take $\beta = 0.1$. Suppose the chosen response scores $-42.0$ under the policy and $-45.0$ under the reference, a relative gain of $+3.0$; the rejected response scores $-50.0$ under the policy and $-48.0$ under the reference, a relative loss of $-2.0$. Then $m = 0.1 \times (3.0 - (-2.0)) = 0.5$, and the loss is $-\log\sigma(0.5)\approx 0.47$. Note what moved the number: not how likely either response is, but how each one's likelihood shifted *relative to the reference*.

In symbols, each example contains a prompt $x$, a preferred response $y^{+}$, and a rejected response $y^{-}$. DPO uses a trainable policy $\pi_\theta$ and a frozen reference $\pi_{ref}$, sums token log-probabilities over each response conditioned on the prompt, and compares how much more the policy favours each response than the reference already did:

$$
m=\beta\left[
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^{+}\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y^{+}\mid x)}-
\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y^{-}\mid x)}{\pi_{ref}(y^{-}\mid x)}
\right],\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma(m).
$$

## Setting up a DPO run on Qwen3.8-27B

Now make it concrete on this course's model. You want to adapt Qwen3.8-27B's instruct-mode style for a support product, and you have 8,000 preference pairs. Setting up the run means answering four questions, in this order.

**First, where does the reference model come from?** Naively loading two 27B bf16 checkpoints is not a one-card plan. Combine DPO with LoRA from [lesson 6.3](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.3-lora-qlora-and-peft/) instead. Train adapters over one low-bit backbone, and obtain the reference log-probability by running the same backbone with adapters disabled. That removes the second weight copy, but it does not prove the run fits a given GPU. Activation memory, quantization metadata, sequence length, micro-batch, and backend buffers still decide how much memory it needs.

**Second, which mode are you training?** Qwen3.8-27B ships thinking mode on by default and instruct mode behind enable_thinking set to False. The two render the same conversation into different token sequences. Log-probabilities are computed over tokens, so a pair collected in one mode and scored in the other is not merely noisy: it is a different objective. Pick a mode, render both the chosen and rejected responses through that exact template branch, and record which branch the dataset used.

**Third, which tokens enter the sum?** The margin is a difference of two log-ratios, policy over reference, one per response. If the policy and the reference agreed on both responses the margin would be zero at any length, so length alone does not force a nonzero margin; it enters only because the policy moves away from the reference. Suppose you are training thinking mode, your chosen response carries a 900-token reasoning trace, and the rejected one carries 200. Each token adds its own log-ratio to its response's total, so the chosen side carries 4.5 times as many terms. Once the policy starts moving, small per-token shifts spread over 900 tokens can outweigh everything that happens in the 200-token response and in both final answers, and the loss can then reward changing the trace more than the answer. Whether that happens depends on how the per-token log-ratios actually move, so log the margin split by segment rather than assume it either way. Either mask the thinking segment out of the loss and score only the final answer, or length-control your pairs deliberately. Do not leave it to chance.

**Fourth, how were the candidates sampled?** Record the full sampling configuration. To reproduce the model card's recommendations, thinking uses temperature 1.0, top_p 0.95, top_k 20, min_p 0.0, presence_penalty 0.0, and repetition_penalty 1.0; instruct uses 0.7, 0.80, 20, 0.0, 1.5, and 1.0. The deployment operator can choose other settings, so samples drawn with other settings are neither invalid in themselves nor necessarily responses users will never see. What matters is whether the candidate distribution covers the behavior you intend to deploy, and whether that choice is recorded.

## Why a falling chosen log-probability is not a bug

The "relative to the reference" distinction sets up the classic wrong turn. Teams log the policy's raw log-probability of the chosen responses and watch it fall during training. They conclude the run is broken: the model is getting worse at producing the answers we told it to prefer.

Look at both series and the panic dissolves. It is entirely normal for chosen log-probability to drift down from $-42$ to $-44$ while rejected log-probability collapses from $-50$ to $-60$. The margin widened from 8 to 16, which is exactly what the objective optimizes. DPO never promised to raise the chosen response's absolute likelihood. It promised to make the chosen response more characteristic of the new policy than the rejected one is. If you want a floor on absolute likelihood, you need a supervised term as well, which is the design ORPO adopts in [lesson 6.7](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.7-orpo-kto-simpo-and-the-alignment-zoo/).

What you should watch instead:

- the margin;
- the pairwise accuracy (how often the chosen response wins);
- the mean response length;
- the KL-like divergence from the reference.

When chosen log-probability falls off a cliff and length collapses with it, the run really is degenerating.

## What DPO does not fix

:::caveat
DPO is not universally "better than RLHF". It solves one preference-learning problem with a simpler objective. PPO remains the tool when rewards are programmatic, interactive, multi-step, or discovered through online exploration, and [lesson 6.8](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.8-grpo-and-rl-on-verifiable-rewards/) shows a third path when outcomes can be checked outright.
:::

Removing the reward model does not remove the preferences. Every weakness lesson 6.4 catalogued survives intact: annotator disagreement, length bias, thin prompt coverage, stale candidate policies, mislabelled examples. DPO can also punish a rejected response that contained useful material, because the label applies to the whole sequence and never identifies the failing span.

Standard DPO is also offline: it collects no new judgments as the policy moves. If training pushes the model into behaviour the dataset never covered, no feedback exists there at all. Iterative variants regenerate candidates and gather new pairs. That restores a data-production loop while still avoiding PPO's machinery.

The mechanical details deserve the same care as the mathematics. Padding must be masked. Prompt tokens must be excluded consistently on both sides. Truncation must never delete the evidence that determined the label: a rejected response cut off before its error is a rejected response with no visible reason to be rejected. And $\beta$ conventions differ across libraries, so a value copied from someone else's config may mean something else in yours.

The durable model is pairwise policy shaping. DPO makes the desired response more characteristic of the new policy relative to a fixed baseline, and the rejected response less so. It trades a learned scalar judge and an online optimizer for an elegant offline loss. The human judgments, the reference choice, the template branch, and the evaluation remain the real specification.
