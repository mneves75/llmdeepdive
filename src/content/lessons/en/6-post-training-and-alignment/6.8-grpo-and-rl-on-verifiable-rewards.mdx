## Why a checker is stronger evidence than a judge

Ask a reward model ([lesson 6.4](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.4-reward-models-and-human-preference-data/)) whether a patch fixes a bug, and you get a learned judgment. Run the repository's test suite against the patch, and you get a reproducible pass/fail result under that suite. That difference, a learned estimate versus an executable observation with an explicit boundary, is the subject of this lesson. It is also why the strongest published claims about coding and mathematics now come from systems trained against checkers rather than judges. A passing suite is stronger evidence than an opinion, but it is not proof that the patch is correct beyond the behaviors the tests cover.

**Verifiable rewards** replace "does this sound good?" with a narrower question whose answer repeats:

- A math expression can be normalized and compared against a known result.
- Code can run against tests in a **sandbox**, an isolated environment that limits what the code can touch.
- A formal proof can be machine-checked.
- A game has rules and a score.

Reinforcement learning fits this setting naturally. The model can sample many candidate solutions and be told which worked, without any human writing the desired reasoning path. A correct solution earns reward even when it takes a route no demonstration contained. That gives the policy (the model being trained) room to discover strategies instead of only imitating one.

## How GRPO scores a group of attempts

**Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO), from the 2024 DeepSeekMath work, samples a group of outputs for the same prompt and grades each one against the others. Each output's **advantage**, the "better or worse than expected" signal from [lesson 6.5](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.5-rlhf-with-ppo/), is its reward minus the group's mean reward, divided by the group's standard deviation (a measure of how spread out the rewards are around their mean).

Put numbers in it. Sample a group of eight attempts at one bug-fix task and run each through the test suite. Two attempts pass and get a reward of 1; the other six fail and get 0. The mean is 0.25. The population standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.1875}\approx 0.433$. So each successful attempt gets an advantage of $(1-0.25)/0.433 \approx +1.73$, and each failure gets $(0-0.25)/0.433 \approx -0.58$.

Two samples are pushed up hard and six are pushed down gently. No learned value model was consulted anywhere: the group's own mean *was* the baseline, which is what replaces PPO's value network. Notice also that the advantages land on a sensible scale whether this task's rewards were 0 and 1 or 0 and 100. That is what makes batches of mixed difficulty trainable.

In symbols, let the group's rewards be $R_1,\ldots,R_G$. The group-relative advantage standardizes each sample against its peers:

$$
A_i=\frac{R_i-\operatorname{mean}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)}
{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)+\epsilon}.
$$

## Why groups that all fail teach nothing

Now the failure that follows from the same formula. Suppose the task is hard and all eight attempts fail. Every reward is 0, the mean is 0, the standard deviation is 0, and every advantage is 0. (The small constant ε in the denominator only keeps that division defined.) The group cost you eight full generations and produced no reward gradient whatsoever. The objective in the DeepSeekMath paper also subtracts a KL penalty, weighted by β, that keeps the policy near the reference. That term still pulls the policy back wherever it has drifted, so the update is exactly zero only when β is 0, but nothing in that pull comes from the task. Flip it around, a task so easy all eight succeed, and you get the same nothing.

This is where the classic wrong turn happens. Seeing training plateau, a team pushes harder problems into the mix, reasoning that the model should be challenged. Throughput collapses, because a growing fraction of the compute budget is spent on all-fail groups that contribute nothing.

The correct move is the opposite of intuition. You want problems where the policy succeeds *sometimes*, because contrast within the group is the entire learning signal. In practice:

- Curate a difficulty curriculum (an ordering of problems by difficulty) around the current success rate.
- Track the fraction of groups with non-zero variance as a first-class training metric.
- Raise difficulty only while that fraction stays healthy.

Group size, sampling temperature, and prompt selection are all levers on the same quantity.

## What the Qwen3.8-27B coding scores show, and what they do not

The payoff for getting this right is the class of agentic coding results now being published: scores for models that edit code and run tools over many steps. Qwen3.8-27B's model card reports SWE-bench Pro 61.7, alongside Terminal Bench 2.1 at 73.0 and DeepSWE 1.1 at 42.2.

Every one of those figures is vendor-reported and awaits independent reproduction. That caveat is worth stating plainly rather than burying, for two reasons. Agentic benchmarks are unusually sensitive to the harness configuration (the code that runs the model on the tasks), the retry policy, and the scaffolding around the model. And no technical report accompanies this model to explain how the numbers were produced.

Notice too that the spread between 61.7 and 42.2 across two coding benchmarks is far too large to treat any single score as "the model's coding ability". What the model card does establish is the shape of the claim. These are outcome scores from executable checkers, not preference scores from a judge. That is a materially stronger kind of evidence than a win rate against another model's prose. The card does not say how the model was trained, so these scores say nothing about whether GRPO or any other method produced them.

## Where verifiable rewards still break

Verifiability is not the same as a correct solution, though:

- An exact-answer checker accepts lucky guesses and reasoning built on invalid intermediate claims.
- Public unit tests can be hard-coded against.
- A code agent will exploit filesystem, timing, or network access if the sandbox permits it.
- A proof checker validates syntax and inference rules, never whether the theorem is the one the user wanted.

Reward design has to state where the real boundary lies, and then defend it.

:::warning
Never execute generated code with production credentials or unrestricted host access. During RL on verifiable rewards the policy is actively searching for anything that raises reward, which means it is actively searching for holes in your sandbox. A verifier is an adversarial security boundary, not an evaluation helper that happens to run code.
:::

Rewards are usually composite: correctness, format validity, tool-use constraints, sometimes a learned style score. Their weights create incentives, and the incentives are rarely what you intended. A formatting reward can drown a sparse correctness signal. A length penalty can suppress the reasoning the task required. A learned judge folded into the mix reintroduces every hazard from lesson 6.4. Log each component separately, always, so that a rising total can never conceal a falling primary success rate.

**Reward overfitting** is the other durable risk: narrow prompt templates and narrow checkers teach superficial strategies that evaporate on fresh distributions. Hold out problem generators rather than individual problems, mutate tests, include adversarial cases, and evaluate final answers and reasoning independently. Contamination, training problems leaking into evaluation sets, is especially corrosive here, because a memorized answer looks exactly like an improvement in reasoning.

GRPO is one member of a wider family, and the acronym should not obscure the principle: sample a group, estimate which members did relatively well, update conservatively. It is compelling wherever a high-precision checker can supply feedback at scale. It is unsuitable wherever the desired outcome is plural, value-laden, or resistant to a robust programmatic test.

The durable model is a tournament with an audited referee. The group creates local competition, the verifier scores it, and optimization increases the probability of winners. The learning is only as good as the referee's grasp of what users actually care about.
