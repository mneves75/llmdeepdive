## Why the model has to search for better answers

Supervised fine-tuning ([lesson 6.2](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.2-supervised-fine-tuning-and-instruction-data/)) can only teach the model to reproduce answers somebody already wrote. If you want it to find a better answer than anything in your dataset, it has to generate candidates itself and be told which ones worked. That is a reinforcement-learning problem: learning by trial and reward instead of by copying. **Reinforcement learning from human feedback** (RLHF) is the classical way of posing it for language models:

- The prompt, plus the tokens generated so far, is the **state**.
- Each generated token is an **action**.
- A completed response earns a **reward**, predicted by the reward model of [lesson 6.4](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.4-reward-models-and-human-preference-data/) from human preferences.

## How one RLHF iteration works

A canonical pipeline runs pretraining, then SFT, then a reward model trained on preference pairs, then optimization of a copy of the SFT model. That copy is the **policy**, the model whose choices are being trained. Each iteration does three things:

1. For a batch (a group processed together) of prompts, the current policy samples responses.
2. The reward model scores them.
3. Those scores become **returns** (the total reward each response earned), which are converted into **advantages**: estimates of how much better an action was than the policy's own expected baseline.

Language generation makes credit assignment hard, meaning it is hard to tell which choices earned the reward. The reward usually arrives once, after the full response, yet hundreds of token choices contributed to it. A learned **value function**, a second network, estimates the expected future reward at each position. Each step gives one error: compare the value estimate at a position with what that step actually delivered, its reward plus the (slightly discounted) estimate at the next position. If the estimate said 0.5 and the step delivered 0.7, the error is +0.2, a little better than expected. Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) then combines these step-by-step errors into an advantage per token, with a setting that trades bias (systematic error) against variance (noise). These details matter: noisy advantages make large-model optimization unstable in ways that look like model problems.

## How PPO's clip shapes the incentive

**Proximal Policy Optimization** (PPO) lets you reuse data sampled from a slightly older copy of the policy. For each token it compares two probabilities: the probability the new policy gives that token, and the probability the old one gave it. Their quotient is the **ratio**. PPO then caps how much credit a sample can earn by moving that ratio far from 1, using a clip range written $\epsilon$.

Work the clip through with numbers, because its asymmetry surprises people. Take a clip range of $\epsilon=0.2$, so the clipped copy of the ratio lies between 0.8 and 1.2.

- **Positive advantage.** Suppose one token had probability 0.20 under the old policy and 0.30 under the new one, giving $\rho_t = 1.5$, and its advantage was $+2.0$. The unclipped term is 3.0, the clipped term is 1.2 times 2.0, or 2.4, and the minimum of the two is 2.4. The surrogate objective (the stand-in objective PPO maximizes) gains nothing further by pushing this token's probability higher, so that sample's incentive to keep climbing disappears. The ratio itself is still 1.5: PPO did not clamp it to 1.2.
- **Negative advantage.** Keep the same ratio but make the advantage $-2.0$. The unclipped term is $-3.0$, the clipped term is $-2.4$, and the minimum is $-3.0$. The clip does not flatten the objective in this direction: a bad action whose probability has risen keeps being pushed down hard.

Other samples share the same parameters, so their gradients can move this ratio farther outside the nominal range. PPO clips an objective contribution, not the optimizer step or the resulting policy. In symbols, with $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ the probability the policy with weights $\theta$ gives action $a_t$ in state $s_t$, and $A_t$ the advantage:

$$
\rho_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)},\qquad
J_t=\min\!\big(\rho_t A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\big).
$$

## What the reference policy and the KL penalty do

RLHF adds a second anchor beyond clipping: a frozen **reference policy**, usually the SFT checkpoint. A penalty based on the KL divergence (a measure of how different two probability distributions are) discourages the optimized model from drifting away from it.

The two controls do different jobs. Clipping reshapes the local incentive in one update; KL penalizes drift accumulated over the whole run. Neither is a hard bound. Without the KL term, the policy can sacrifice fluency, diversity, and general competence to exploit peculiarities of the reward model. A controller may adjust the penalty coefficient to keep KL within a target band, but that band is an engineering choice, not a constant of nature.

## Why rising reward is not success

Here is the classic wrong turn, and almost everyone takes it once. The dashboard shows mean reward climbing from 0.9 to 1.3 over a training run, roughly a 40 percent improvement, and the run is declared a success. Lesson 6.4 already dismantled this. The reward model's pairwise loss depends only on score differences, so the scale has no unit and the level has no meaning. Worse, the quantity going up is measured by exactly the model the policy is learning to game.

The correction is to treat training reward as a diagnostic and never as an outcome. Judge the run on three kinds of evidence:

- held-out human comparisons collected after training;
- task checks that were never part of the optimized signal;
- the control variables: KL against the reference, mean response length, refusal rate, sample diversity.

A run where reward rose 40 percent while length rose 60 percent and KL doubled did not get better; it got longer and stranger.

:::warning
PPO stabilizes policy updates. It does not validate the reward. A flawlessly implemented optimizer is a very effective machine for exploiting a misspecified reward model, and the better your engineering, the faster it finds the exploit.
:::

## What the loop costs, and how it breaks

The loop is also expensive in a way that excludes most teams before they start. A full PPO setup can hold four models at once (policy, frozen reference, reward, and value) plus optimizer state and generated rollouts, the sampled responses. Sizing that for a 27B-class model is sobering. The policy alone is 54 GB in bf16, the reference is another 54 GB, and AdamW state for 27B parameters runs somewhere between 324 and 432 GB. Sampling is part of training, so throughput depends on your inference stack as much as on backpropagation.

Because so many components move together, bugs hide well:

- mismatched padding masks (the markers that tell the model which positions are filler);
- terminal rewards attached to the wrong position;
- stale old log-probabilities;
- a chat template applied during sampling but not during scoring.

Any of these makes the run optimize a different objective than the one you wrote down, with no error to warn you.

The visible symptoms are consistent across teams. Reward rises while independent quality falls. Response length inflates. Phrasing becomes repetitive. Refusals multiply. KL collapses toward zero, meaning nothing is being learned, or explodes, meaning the policy has left the language distribution. Evaluate with judgments and checks that were never part of the optimized signal, or you are grading an exam with the answer sheet the student learned to forge.

PPO remains important because it can optimize arbitrary signals scored on a whole sequence and explore online (by sampling new responses as it trains), which no offline method can. It is also sensitive to many hyperparameters across many moving parts. Two later families change the trade-off:

- Direct preference methods ([lesson 6.6](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.6-dpo-skipping-the-reward-model/)) delete the reward model and the RL loop for a class of problems, trading flexibility for simplicity.
- Verifiable-reward methods ([lesson 6.8](/lessons/6-post-training-and-alignment/6.8-grpo-and-rl-on-verifiable-rewards/)) keep the reinforcement learning but replace the learned judge with a programmatic checker, which changes the risk profile entirely.

Whichever you pick, the mental model holds. The reward proposes a direction. The value model reduces variance. Clipping limits the incentive to keep moving in selected directions. KL penalizes drift. None of it hard-bounds the policy, and none of it answers whether "higher reward" still means "better for the person reading the output".
