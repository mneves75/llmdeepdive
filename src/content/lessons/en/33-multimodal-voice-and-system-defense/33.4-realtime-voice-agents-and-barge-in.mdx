A text chatbot can take 1.2 seconds before its first word appears and still feel responsive. A voice agent taking 1.2 seconds feels broken. Human conversation has a tight physical rhythm: speakers exchange turns with roughly **200 to 500 ms** of gap. Once silence stretches past 700–1,000 ms, callers assume the line dropped, repeat "Hello?", and collide head-on with the bot's delayed reply.

This lesson applies the latency budgeting discipline of [lesson 31.1](/lessons/31-ai-system-design-and-caching/31.1-end-to-end-latency-budgets/) to the speech representations of [lesson 33.3](/lessons/33-multimodal-voice-and-system-defense/33.3-audio-tokens-whisper-stt-and-tts/), proving with exact arithmetic why cascaded voice pipelines fail the 500 ms test and how streaming speech-to-speech, WebRTC, and barge-in engineering make voice agents feel natural.

## Worked example: 1,170 ms cascaded vs 460 ms native streaming

Every voice turn is a serial chain of delays from the moment the caller finishes speaking until the first millisecond of the bot's reply hits their ear:

$$
T_{\text{turn}} = T_{\text{VAD}} + T_{\text{STT}} + T_{\text{LLM}} + T_{\text{TTS}} + T_{\text{RTT}}
$$

First price a typical **cascaded voice pipeline** (separate Speech-to-Text, text LLM, and Text-to-Speech services wired together):

- **VAD endpointing** ($T_{\text{VAD}} = 300\ \text{ms}$): Voice Activity Detection waits 300 ms of silence to confirm the caller actually stopped talking rather than pausing between words.
- **STT finalization** ($T_{\text{STT}} = 220\ \text{ms}$): the speech recognizer finalizes the trailing words into a stable text transcript.
- **LLM TTFT** ($T_{\text{LLM}} = 380\ \text{ms}$): the language model prefills the conversation history and emits enough initial tokens to form the first speakable clause.
- **TTS first audio** ($T_{\text{TTS}} = 180\ \text{ms}$): the speech synthesizer turns that first clause into the opening audio frame.
- **Network RTT** ($T_{\text{RTT}} = 90\ \text{ms}$): audio packets travel up to the server and back to the caller.

Sum the cascaded chain:

$$
T_{\text{cascaded}} = 300 + 220 + 380 + 180 + 90 = 1{,}170\ \text{ms}
$$

At **1,170 ms**, the bot sits silent for more than a full second on every single turn—even before adding any tool call or RAG lookup.

Now price a **native streaming speech-to-speech pipeline** over WebRTC, where one multimodal model ingests streaming audio tokens and emits streaming audio tokens directly (removing separate STT finalization and TTS first-chunk hops):

- **Semantic VAD endpointing** ($T_{\text{VAD}} = 180\ \text{ms}$): a lightweight turn-detection model uses intonation and syntax—not just raw silence duration—to spot finished thoughts earlier.
- **Native audio-to-audio first frame** ($T_{\text{LLM}} = 210\ \text{ms}$, with $T_{\text{STT}} = 0$ and $T_{\text{TTS}} = 0$): the model generates the opening neural audio codec frames directly.
- **WebRTC transport RTT** ($T_{\text{RTT}} = 70\ \text{ms}$): direct edge-routed UDP media path.

Sum the native streaming chain:

$$
T_{\text{streaming}} = 180 + 0 + 210 + 0 + 70 = 460\ \text{ms}
$$

Native streaming drops turn silence to **460 ms**—under the **500 ms** conversational target, saving **710 ms** ($1{,}170 - 460 = 710\ \text{ms}$) on every turn.

## Endpointing: silence timers versus semantic turn detection

Notice that **VAD endpointing** alone burned 300 ms in the cascaded stack. Why not just lower the silence threshold to 80 ms? Because humans pause for 200–400 ms right in the middle of a sentence ("My order number is... let me look... 4829"). A naive silence timer interrupts the user every time they breathe or think.

**Semantic endpointing** solves that tradeoff by looking at acoustic prosody (falling pitch at the end of a question versus flat pitch mid-phrase) alongside partial words ("My order number is..." is grammatically incomplete). When the utterance is complete and pitch drops, the model triggers at ~180 ms; when the sentence is clearly unfinished, it holds its silence patiently for 800–1,200 ms without cutting the caller off.

## Transport: why WebRTC beats WebSockets for voice

Many prototypes stream microphone audio to the backend over a **WebSocket** because WebSockets are easy to wire in Node or Python. On clean office Wi-Fi, WebSockets look fine. On a real mobile phone on 4G/5G with 2% packet loss, WebSockets degrade fast.

WebSockets run over **TCP**, which enforces strict in-order delivery. If audio packet #41 drops, TCP freezes packets #42, #43, and #44 in the kernel buffer until #41 is retransmitted—creating **head-of-line blocking** that turns a 20 ms hiccup into a 350 ms stutter and breaks real-time turn-taking.

**WebRTC** (W3C WebRTC 1.0) runs real-time media over **UDP** with three built-in audio features you would otherwise have to hand-roll:

- **Packet-loss concealment (Opus codec)**: a single lost 20 ms packet is interpolated locally rather than stalling newer packets behind it.
- **Adaptive jitter buffers**: smooths uneven wireless arrival times without growing multi-hundred-millisecond queues.
- **Acoustic Echo Cancellation (AEC)**: subtracts the bot's own speaker output from the user's microphone input so the bot does not hear its own voice and interrupt itself.

## Barge-in and playback-state truncation

In real phone calls, people interrupt constantly ("Actually, cancel that and check Tuesday instead"). Supporting **barge-in** (letting the user interrupt the agent mid-sentence) requires tight coordination across client and server:

1. **Immediate local mute (< 50 ms)**: the moment local VAD (after AEC) detects user speech, the client stops audio playback and flushes its queued audio frames.
2. **Server stream cancellation**: the client sends a `barge_in` event with the exact `played_ms` offset; the server immediately aborts the active LLM/TTS stream so you stop paying for unspoken tokens.
3. **History truncation**: because generation runs far faster than 1x real-time speech, the LLM may have generated 8 seconds of reply while the user only heard 1.5 seconds before interrupting. The server must trim the stored assistant transcript down to the 1.5-second mark. Without truncation, the agent later behaves as if it already told the user facts they never heard.

:::warning
Never ignore Acoustic Echo Cancellation when enabling barge-in on speakerphones. Without AEC, the bot's own voice leaks into the microphone, trips VAD as "user speech," and triggers an endless self-interruption loop after the first syllable.
:::

## Keeping tool calls and compliance intact

Native speech-to-speech models hit 460 ms effortlessly on pure dialogue, yet enterprise workflows still require RAG lookups, database writes, and auditable transcripts. Two practical patterns bridge both worlds:

- **Filler acknowledgement during slow tools**: when a tool call takes 900 ms (checking a flight or order API), emit a brief, natural acknowledgement ("Checking that order now") before executing the tool asynchronously so callers do not sit in dead air.
- **Dual-channel transcript and guardrail logging**: log the synchronized text transcript alongside OpenTelemetry audio-turn spans (including `vad_ms`, `first_audio_ms`, and `barge_in_count`) so compliance reviews and offline evals still work identically to text agents.

:::caveat
Cascaded STT + LLM + TTS pipelines remain the right choice when you must enforce strict deterministic guardrails, exact PII redaction, or domain-specific pronunciation dictionaries *between* user speech and model execution—or when your budget requires routing simple queries to a cheap text LLM. Optimize cascaded pipelines aggressively with streaming STT partials, clause-level TTS streaming, and WebRTC transport, which can pull cascaded latency down toward 650–800 ms.
:::
