A text chatbot can take 1.2 seconds before its first word appears and still feel responsive. A voice agent taking 1.2 seconds feels broken. Human conversation runs on a tight physical rhythm: speakers exchange turns with roughly **200 to 500 ms** of gap. Once silence stretches past 700–1,000 ms, callers assume the line dropped, repeat "Hello?", and talk right over the bot's delayed reply.

This lesson applies the latency budgeting discipline of [lesson 31.1](/lessons/31-ai-system-design-and-caching/31.1-end-to-end-latency-budgets/) to the speech representations of [lesson 33.3](/lessons/33-multimodal-voice-and-system-defense/33.3-audio-tokens-whisper-stt-and-tts/), proving with exact arithmetic why cascaded voice pipelines fail the 500 ms test and how streaming speech-to-speech, WebRTC, and barge-in engineering keep voice agents responsive.

## Worked example: 1,170 ms cascaded vs 460 ms native streaming

Every voice turn is a serial chain of delays from the moment the caller finishes speaking until the first millisecond of the bot's reply reaches their ear:

$$
T_{\text{turn}} = T_{\text{VAD}} + T_{\text{STT}} + T_{\text{LLM}} + T_{\text{TTS}} + T_{\text{RTT}}
$$

In a typical **cascaded voice pipeline**, five separate delays stack in order: **Voice Activity Detection (VAD)** waits for silence to confirm the caller finished; **Speech-to-Text (STT)** finalizes the last words into a stable transcript; the **Large Language Model (LLM)** reads the conversation history (**prefill**) and reaches its **Time to First Token (TTFT)** plus enough words for a speakable clause; **Text-to-Speech (TTS)** renders the opening sound chunk; and **network Round-Trip Time (RTT)** carries audio packets to the server and back. Using the stage timings in the figure below:

$$
T_{\text{cascaded}} = 300 + 220 + 380 + 180 + 90 = 1{,}170\ \text{ms}
$$

At **1,170 ms**, the bot sits silent for more than a full second on every turn—before adding any tool call or Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG, external document search) lookup.

A **native streaming speech-to-speech pipeline** over **WebRTC** (Web Real-Time Communication, the browser standard for live audio and video calls) collapses the separate STT and TTS hops ($T_{\text{STT}} = 0$ and $T_{\text{TTS}} = 0$) because one multimodal model reads streaming audio tokens and emits streaming audio tokens directly. Pairing **semantic VAD endpointing** (using intonation and grammar, not just silence) with an edge-routed (nearby regional server) WebRTC path gives:

$$
T_{\text{streaming}} = 180 + 0 + 210 + 0 + 70 = 460\ \text{ms}
$$

Native streaming drops turn silence to **460 ms**—under the **500 ms** conversational ceiling, saving **710 ms** ($1{,}170 - 460 = 710\ \text{ms}$) on every turn. Compare both stacks directly in the figure below.

## Endpointing: silence timers versus semantic turn detection

VAD endpointing alone burned 300 ms in the cascaded stack. Why not lower the silence threshold to 80 ms? Because humans pause for 200–400 ms in the middle of a sentence ("My order number is... let me look... 4829"). A fixed silence timer cuts the caller off every time they breathe or think.

**Semantic endpointing** resolves that trade-off by reading acoustic **prosody** (falling pitch at the end of a thought versus flat pitch mid-phrase) alongside partial words ("My order number is..." is grammatically unfinished). When the utterance is complete and pitch drops, the model triggers at about 180 ms; when the sentence is clearly unfinished, it waits patiently for 800–1,200 ms without interrupting.

## Transport: why WebRTC beats WebSockets for voice

Many prototypes stream microphone audio to a server over a **WebSocket** (a persistent two-way connection between browser and server) because WebSockets are easy to set up in Node or Python server code. On clean office Wi-Fi, WebSockets sound fine. On a mobile phone over 4G/5G with 2% packet loss, they degrade quickly.

WebSockets run over **Transmission Control Protocol (TCP)**, which enforces strict in-order delivery. If audio packet #41 drops, TCP holds packets #42, #43, and #44 in the operating system's **kernel buffer** (temporary network holding area) until #41 is resent—creating **head-of-line blocking** that turns a 20 ms hiccup into a 350 ms freeze.

**WebRTC** (W3C WebRTC 1.0) sends live media over **User Datagram Protocol (UDP)**—a fast transport that never stalls newer packets to wait for a lost one—with three audio tools built in:

- **Packet-loss concealment (Opus codec)**: the Opus speech compressor fills in a single lost 20 ms packet locally instead of stalling newer audio behind it.
- **Adaptive jitter buffers**: a small shock-absorber queue smooths uneven wireless arrival times without growing multi-hundred-millisecond delays.
- **Acoustic Echo Cancellation (AEC)**: subtracts the bot's own speaker output from the user's microphone input so the bot does not hear its own voice and interrupt itself.

## Barge-in and playback-state truncation

In real phone calls, people interrupt constantly ("Actually, cancel that and check Tuesday instead"). Supporting **barge-in** (letting the caller interrupt the agent mid-sentence) requires three coordinated steps between client and server:

1. **Immediate local mute (< 50 ms)**: the moment local VAD (after AEC) detects user speech, the client stops speaker playback and drops its queued audio frames.
2. **Server stream cancellation**: the client sends a `barge_in` event carrying `played_ms` (the exact millisecond of audio actually played); the server cancels the active LLM/TTS stream immediately so you stop paying for unspoken tokens.
3. **History truncation**: because model generation runs much faster than 1x human speech, the LLM may have generated 8 seconds of reply while the caller heard only 1.5 seconds before interrupting. The server must trim the stored assistant transcript down to the 1.5-second mark, or the agent will later assume it already told the caller facts they never heard.

:::warning
Never omit Acoustic Echo Cancellation when enabling barge-in on speakerphones. Without AEC, the bot's own voice leaks into the microphone, trips VAD as "user speech," and triggers an endless self-interruption loop after the first syllable.
:::

## Keeping tool calls and compliance intact

Native speech-to-speech models stay under the 500 ms budget on pure dialogue, yet enterprise workflows still need RAG lookups, database writes, and auditable transcripts. Two practical patterns bridge both needs:

- **Filler acknowledgement during slow tools**: when a tool call takes 900 ms (checking a flight or order API), speak a short acknowledgement ("Checking that order now") before running the tool asynchronously so callers do not sit in dead air.
- **Dual-channel transcript and guardrail logging**: record the synchronized text transcript beside **OpenTelemetry spans** (standardized timing logs for each step, tracking `vad_ms` for detection delay, `first_audio_ms` for opening sound, and `barge_in_count` for interruptions) so compliance reviews and offline evaluations work just like text agents.

:::caveat
Cascaded STT + LLM + TTS pipelines remain the right choice when you must enforce strict deterministic guardrails, exact redaction of Personally Identifiable Information (PII, such as names or card numbers), or domain pronunciation dictionaries *between* user speech and model execution—or when your budget requires routing routine questions to a cheap text LLM. Optimize cascaded pipelines with streaming STT partials, clause-level TTS streaming, and WebRTC transport, which can pull cascaded latency down toward 650–800 ms.
:::
