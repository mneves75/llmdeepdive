Engineers often treat AI governance as a wall of PDFs handed down by legal two weeks before launch. That approach breaks down for a simple operational reason: legal teams can name obligations, but only engineers can verify whether a vector index isolates tenants, whether a prompt trace strips PII, or whether an evaluation gate actually blocks a broken release. Three frameworks dominate enterprise AI reviews worldwide—the **OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications**, the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework (Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1)**, and **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the EU AI Act)**. Understanding how they divide the problem lets you build one set of verifiable engineering controls that satisfies all three.

## One system, three lenses: threats, lifecycle, and law

Each framework answers a different question:

- **OWASP LLM Top 10 (threat catalog):** Lists the concrete failure modes attackers and bugs trigger in LLM applications, including Prompt Injection (LLM01), Sensitive Information Disclosure (LLM02), Data and Model Poisoning (LLM04), Excessive Agency (LLM06), System Prompt Leakage (LLM07), Vector and Embedding Weaknesses (LLM08), and Misinformation (LLM09).
- **NIST AI 600-1 (engineering risk lifecycle):** Translates generative AI hazards (confabulation, CBRN, data privacy, information security, value-chain risks) into concrete actions across four continuous functions: **Govern** (accountability and policy), **Map** (context and scope), **Measure** (metrics, red-teaming, and testing), and **Manage** (mitigation and incident response).
- **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 — EU AI Act (binding law):** Assigns legal obligations by deployment risk tier rather than by model architecture alone, with phased enforcement running from 2025 through 2028 (prohibited practices and AI literacy first, general-purpose AI and Article 50 transparency obligations in 2025–2026, Annex III high-risk systems from 2 December 2027, and Annex I safety-component high-risk products from 2 August 2028 under the Digital Omnibus on AI).

Notice that the controls you built across this track already map directly onto these frameworks instead of requiring separate bureaucracy.

Walk through a concrete example: an enterprise HR and policy RAG assistant deployed across European and US offices. Suppose an auditor asks how you control unauthorized disclosure, hallucinated legal advice, and data poisoning. The quantitative habit from earlier lessons carries over here too. Suppose your system ships $12$ production changes per quarter across prompts, chunking settings, and base model checkpoints. If each change requires $8$ hours of manual compliance sign-off, governance eats $12 \times 8 = 96$ engineering hours per quarter and invites teams to batch risky changes together. Automating evidence capture inside CI reduces per-release governance verification to minutes while raising accuracy:

$$
\text{Audit Coverage} = \frac{\text{Releases with Versioned Control Evidence}}{\text{Total Production Releases}} = 1.0.
$$

Every single release automatically exports its coupled manifest (model version, prompt hash, index snapshot, guardrail thresholds, and eval pass rates) into an immutable audit log.

## Classifying EU AI Act risk tiers accurately

Under Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, legal obligations scale sharply with **intended use**, not merely with parameter count:

- **Unacceptable risk (prohibited):** Social scoring, untargeted scraping of facial images for recognition databases, workplace or educational emotion recognition (with narrow medical/safety exceptions), and manipulative exploitation of vulnerabilities. Do not build these.
- **High-risk (Annex III and Annex I):** Standalone use cases in **Annex III** (recruitment and HR screening, creditworthiness scoring, critical infrastructure, education grading, law enforcement) face full high-risk obligations from **2 December 2027**, while AI safety components embedded in regulated products under **Annex I** (such as medical devices and machinery) apply from **2 August 2028**. High-risk systems require a documented risk management system, data governance records, technical documentation (Annex IV), automatic event logging, clear instructions for deployers, human oversight mechanisms, and formal conformity assessment before deployment.
- **Limited risk (transparency obligations, Article 50):** Two distinct duties apply: (1) **interaction disclosure** requires customer-facing chatbots to inform humans that they are interacting with an AI system, and (2) **output marking** requires providers of generative systems to mark synthetic **text**, audio, image, and video outputs in a machine-readable format (plus clear disclosure for deepfakes and AI-generated public-interest text).
- **Minimal risk:** Internal code search, spam filters, and developer documentation assistants carry no mandatory Annex III conformity gate, though voluntary codes of conduct and GDPR/security law still apply in full.

:::caveat
Calling a compliant general-purpose LLM API does not make your downstream application automatically compliant. Under the EU AI Act, the organization deploying an AI system for a high-risk purpose (such as resume screening or loan triage) assumes deployer or provider obligations for the integrated application, including its RAG corpus, prompts, and human-oversight workflow.
:::

## Building living governance artifacts in the repository

To keep governance lightweight and honest, store four small, version-controlled artifacts right alongside your application code:

1. **System card (`docs/ai-system-card.md`):** Names the intended use, prohibited out-of-scope uses, risk tier classification, data sources, third-party model vendors (with ZDR/residency status), and named human owners.
2. **Threat-to-control matrix:** Links every applicable OWASP LLM risk to the exact automated test or runtime middleware that enforces it.
3. **CI evidence bundle:** Attaches the latest retrieval recall, grounding faithfulness, red-team attack recall, and benign false-positive rate to every release tag.
4. **Incident and kill-switch runbook:** Documents who can disable tool execution, roll back a poisoned index snapshot, or switch model providers within a defined Service Level Objective (SLO), plus statutory breach-notification timelines.

When governance lives as tested code and release artifacts rather than slide decks, security reviews speed up instead of stalling launches—and every claim you make to customers or regulators is backed by reproducible evidence.
