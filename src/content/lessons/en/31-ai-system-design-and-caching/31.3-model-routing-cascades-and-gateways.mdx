Sending every user query—from "summarize this three-line receipt" to "reconcile a multi-jurisdiction tax clause"—to your most expensive frontier model wastes 60% to 85% of your inference budget and inflates latency across routine traffic. In [lesson 24.4](/lessons/24-agent-resilience-and-fallbacks/24.4-model-cascades-and-budgets/), you built a budget-capped sequential cascade for an invoice agent. In full-scale production systems, teams choose between two complementary dispatch patterns—**upfront model routing** and **sequential cascades**—and operate both behind a centralized **AI gateway**.

## Upfront routing vs. sequential cascades

Both techniques pair a fast, inexpensive model ($c_{\text{small}}$, latency $T_{\text{small}}$) with a capable, expensive frontier model ($c_{\text{large}}$, latency $T_{\text{large}}$), but they decide *when* to escalate at different points:

- **Upfront routing (RouteLLM — Ong et al., 2024):** A lightweight classifier or matrix-factorization router inspects the incoming prompt *before* any LLM runs, predicts whether the small model can match the strong model on this query, and sends fraction $p$ directly to the small model and $(1 - p)$ directly to the frontier model. Ong et al. trained routers on preference datasets (such as Chatbot Arena) and showed cost reductions over $2\times$ on benchmarks like MT-Bench and MMLU while preserving over 95% of frontier quality.
- **Sequential cascade (FrugalGPT — Chen et al., 2023):** Every request goes to the cheap model first. A verifier (a deterministic schema/math check, a groundedness score, or a trained reliability scorer) checks the answer; only the failing fraction $(1 - p)$ escalates to the frontier model.

Compare the exact cost and latency math using small, concrete numbers. Let $c_{\text{small}} = \text{USD } 0.60$ per 1,000 requests, $c_{\text{large}} = \text{USD } 9.00$ per 1,000 requests, $T_{\text{small}} = 900\text{ ms}$, $T_{\text{large}} = 4{,}500\text{ ms}$, router latency $T_{\text{route}} = 20\text{ ms}$, and suppose $p = 0.75$ of queries are handled well by the small model:

- **Frontier only:** Cost = $\text{USD } 9.00 / 1\text{k}$; hard-query latency = $4{,}500\text{ ms}$.
- **Upfront router ($p = 0.75$):**

$$
C_{\text{route}} = p \cdot c_{\text{small}} + (1 - p) \cdot c_{\text{large}} = 0.75 \times 0.60 + 0.25 \times 9.00 = \text{USD } 2.70 / 1\text{k}.
$$

Hard queries pay $T_{\text{route}} + T_{\text{large}} = 4{,}520\text{ ms}$ (only 20 ms overhead).

- **Sequential cascade ($p = 0.75$):**

$$
C_{\text{cascade}} = c_{\text{small}} + (1 - p) \cdot c_{\text{large}} = 0.60 + 0.25 \times 9.00 = \text{USD } 2.85 / 1\text{k}.
$$

Hard queries pay $T_{\text{small}} + T_{\text{large}} = 5{,}400\text{ ms}$ (stacking both turns unless the verifier rejects early during streaming).

The tradeoff is clear. Routing preserves tail latency on hard queries from [lesson 31.1](/lessons/31-ai-system-design-and-caching/31.1-end-to-end-latency-budgets/) and works well when difficulty is predictable from the prompt alone (intent classification, language complexity, query length, presence of multi-hop reasoning cues). Cascades win whenever you possess a **cheap, trusted verifier** of the output—JSON schema validation, unit-test execution, SQL syntax + row check, or citation grounding—because the verifier catches mistakes that no pre-generation router could foresee.

## Calibration: choosing the routing threshold without blind spots

A router outputs a probability score $s(x) \in [0, 1]$ estimating the chance that the frontier model outperforms the small model on prompt $x$, and routes to the frontier model when $s(x) \ge \theta$. Sweeping $\theta$ traces a **cost-quality Pareto curve**:

- Lowering $\theta$ sends more traffic to the frontier model (higher quality, higher spend).
- Raising $\theta$ sends more traffic to the small model (lower spend, steeper quality drop once difficult prompts leak onto the small tier).

Never calibrate $\theta$ on generic public leaderboards alone. A router trained on general chat preferences can score poorly on domain-specific legal citations or internal SQL dialects because distribution shift changes which queries are hard. Collect 500–1,000 real production queries, score both the small and frontier models with your domain eval rubric, and pick the highest $\theta$ that stays inside your acceptable quality delta (for example, within 1 percentage point of frontier-only pass rate). Re-calibrate whenever either model updates.

## What an AI gateway actually owns in production

Once an application talks to multiple models, caches, and providers, scattering SDK calls and retry loops across microservices creates ungovernable spend and inconsistent outages. A centralized **AI gateway** (an internal proxy service sitting between application servers and model providers) consolidates six cross-cutting responsibilities:

- **Unified routing and fallback:** Maps logical model aliases (`support-fast`, `reasoning-high`) to concrete provider endpoints, failing over across regions or secondary providers on HTTP 429/5xx bursts or TTFT circuit-breaker trips.
- **Per-tenant rate limiting and token quotas:** Enforces requests-per-minute (RPM), tokens-per-minute (TPM), and daily dollar caps per tenant so one runaway script cannot exhaust shared provider quotas.
- **Virtual keys and secret isolation:** Issues scoped internal tokens to application services while keeping real provider API keys locked inside the gateway.
- **Semantic/exact caching and PII redaction:** Executes caching and redaction once at the edge instead of duplicating middleware in every service.
- **Normalized telemetry and cost attribution:** Tags every request with `tenant_id`, `feature`, `prompt_version`, `route_decision`, cached vs uncached input tokens, output tokens, and exact USD cost.
- **Shadow traffic and canary splits:** Mirrors a small percentage of live requests to a candidate smaller model or updated router threshold so teams compare real quality before shifting production traffic.

:::caveat
An AI gateway is itself on the critical path of every single LLM call. Keep the gateway lightweight (under 5–10 ms added proxy latency), deploy it redundantly across availability zones, and verify every fallback model against your full eval suite—a blind failover to a weaker model that breaks structured JSON or ignores safety rules causes a silent outage worse than a clean retry.
:::

Combine all three pieces—a stable-prefix cache for repeated context, an upfront router for predictable difficulty splits, and a verified cascade where deterministic checks exist—behind one observed AI gateway, and you turn model selection from a hardcoded constant into an adjustable engineering dial.
