The error audit in [lesson 29.1](/lessons/29-llm-and-rag-evaluations/29.1-error-analysis-and-failure-taxonomies/) showed why a single end-to-end score hides bugs: retrieval failures and generation failures need separate meters. If hybrid search (combining keyword and meaning-based search) from [lesson 27.5](/lessons/27-retrieval-and-hybrid-search/27.5-bm25-hybrid-search-and-rrf/) misses the right passage, no prompt edit can save the reply. If retrieval brings the exact clause and the generator contradicts it, rebuilding the vector index (the searchable map of passage embeddings) wastes a week. This lesson derives the core information-retrieval (IR) metrics from one worked run—matching the interactive calculator on this page—and pairs them with generation-side grounding metrics.

## One worked retrieval run by hand

Suppose a query has $R = 5$ genuinely relevant chunks (short passages split from source documents) anywhere in the corpus (the full searchable document library). Your retriever returns 8 candidate chunks, and human relevance labels show hits at **ranks 2, 4, 5, and 8** (`[miss, hit, miss, hit, hit, miss, miss, hit]`). Because your context assembly budget keeps only the top $k = 5$ chunks, evaluate the list at cutoff (maximum kept position) $k = 5$.

Count the hits inside the top 5 slots first: ranks 2, 4, and 5 are relevant, while rank 8 falls outside the cutoff, giving $\text{Hits@5} = 3$.

1. **Recall@5** measures what fraction of *all* relevant corpus chunks made it into the top $k$ window:
   $$
   \text{Recall@5} = \frac{3}{5} = 60.0\%.
   $$
2. **Precision@5** measures what fraction of the $k$ returned slots are actually relevant (useful passages versus irrelevant noise inside the prompt):
   $$
   \text{Precision@5} = \frac{3}{5} = 60.0\%.
   $$
3. **Reciprocal Rank (RR)** looks strictly at the rank $r_1$ of the *first* relevant hit across the full returned list (averaging RR across a query set $Q$ yields **Mean Reciprocal Rank, MRR**). Here the first hit sits at rank 2:
   $$
   \text{RR} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.500.
   $$
4. **Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG@5)** rewards every relevant hit inside top $k$ while discounting deeper ranks by $1 / \log_2(\text{rank} + 1)$ (using the base-2 logarithm so weight drops smoothly as rank grows instead of cutting off abruptly). Rank 1 gets weight $1/\log_2(2) = 1.000$, rank 2 gets $1/\log_2(3) \approx 0.631$, rank 3 gets $1/\log_2(4) = 0.500$, rank 4 gets $1/\log_2(5) \approx 0.431$, and rank 5 gets $1/\log_2(6) \approx 0.387$. Summing our hits at ranks 2, 4, and 5 gives:
   $$
   \text{DCG@5} = \frac{1}{\log_2(3)} + \frac{1}{\log_2(5)} + \frac{1}{\log_2(6)} = 0.631 + 0.431 + 0.387 = 1.448.
   $$
5. **Ideal DCG (IDCG@5)** is the maximum possible DCG@5 if all $\min(R, k) = 5$ relevant chunks sat at ranks 1 through 5:
   $$
   \text{IDCG@5} = 1 + 0.631 + 0.500 + 0.431 + 0.387 = 2.948.
   $$
6. **Normalized DCG (NDCG@5)** divides actual DCG@5 by IDCG@5 so scores stay between 0 and 1 across queries with different numbers of relevant chunks:
   $$
   \text{NDCG@5} = \frac{1.448}{2.948} = 0.491.
   $$

## The general retrieval formulas and when each matters

Across an evaluation set $Q$ of queries, let $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ denote whether rank $i$ is relevant for a query with $R$ total relevant chunks in the corpus, and let $r_1$ be the rank of the first relevant hit:

$$
\text{Recall@}k = \frac{1}{R}\sum_{i=1}^{k} y_i, \qquad \text{Precision@}k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} y_i, \qquad \text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{q \in Q}\frac{1}{r_1(q)},
$$

$$
\text{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{y_i}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \qquad \text{NDCG@}k = \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k}.
$$

Each metric guards a different stage of a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipeline (Lewis et al., 2020; Khattab & Zaharia, 2020):

- **Stage-1 hybrid search optimizes Recall@50 (or Recall@20):** if a relevant chunk misses the stage-1 candidate pool, no downstream reranker (second-stage sorting model) can recover it.
- **Stage-2 reranking optimizes MRR and NDCG@5:** once the right chunks are in the pool, pushing them into top slots protects against truncation (cutting off passages past the prompt limit) and lost-in-the-middle dilution (when a model overlooks facts buried in the middle of long context).
- **Multi-fact synthesis tracks Recall@k at the final prompt cutoff:** when an answer requires three separate policy clauses, MRR can hit 1.000 from the first clause alone while Recall@5 exposes that the other two clauses never arrived.

## Generation metrics: faithfulness versus answer correctness

Once retrieval puts the right evidence into the prompt, grade generation along two separate dimensions (mirroring the support and utility checks in Asai et al., 2023):

- **Faithfulness (groundedness):** break the generated answer into atomic claims (single verifiable statements) and measure what share of claims are directly supported by the retrieved chunks. A model that invents a fee amount fails faithfulness even if that fee happens to be true in the real world.
- **Answer correctness and relevance:** verify whether the answer actually addresses the user's question and matches the ground-truth (human-verified) reference answer or rubric. A reply that faithfully quotes an unrelated refund paragraph scores 100% on faithfulness and 0% on answer correctness.
- **Abstention precision and recall:** on unanswerable queries, track both how often the model abstains (refuses to guess) when evidence is missing and how rarely it refuses answerable queries.

## Reading retrieval and generation scores together

Putting both halves in one table turns guesswork into diagnosis:

| Recall@5 | Faithfulness | Answer correctness | Likely root cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low (45%) | High (96%) | Low (42%) | Retrieval or chunking (document splitting) bottleneck; generator is obeying bad context |
| High (92%) | Low (64%) | Low (58%) | Grounding failure; tighten citations, context order, or model strength |
| High (92%) | High (96%) | Low (60%) | Prompt or task mismatch; model summarizes faithfully without answering the question |

:::note
Always report $k$ and corpus size alongside every retrieval metric. "Recall = 85%" means nothing until you know whether $k = 5$ or $k = 50$, and whether the relevance labels came from real user queries or easy synthetic keyword matches.
:::
