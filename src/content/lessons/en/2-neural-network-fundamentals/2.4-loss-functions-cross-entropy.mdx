A model has just read the prefix "The capital of France is" and must produce a number saying how well it did. What number? "Right or wrong" is nearly useless. It cannot distinguish a model that ranked the correct continuation second from one that ranked it two hundred thousandth, and a yes/no flag has no gradient to descend. The scoring rule has to reward probability mass placed on what actually happened, and it has to punish confident mistakes harder than uncertain ones. That rule is cross-entropy, and this lesson makes it concrete at the scale the rest of the course works in.

## What does a loss function have to measure?

A **loss function** turns model output and target into a single scalar (one number) that optimization can reduce. For classification the network emits one **logit** $z_i$ per class: an unrestricted score, not a probability. Softmax, met in [lesson 0.3](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.3-probability-you-need/), converts the logits into a distribution:

$$
p_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}.
$$

Adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, so only relative scores matter. Suppose the target is **one-hot**: all of its probability sits on the single class $y$ that was observed. Cross-entropy then charges the negative logarithm of the probability the model gave that class. With the natural logarithm, a model that gave the observed token probability 0.5 pays about 0.69; one that gave it 0.01 pays about 4.61. In symbols:

$$
L=-\log p_y.
$$

Probability near one gives loss near zero; probability near zero gives an enormous loss. The asymmetry is the useful part. With the natural logarithm the loss is measured in nats, the unit [lesson 1.7](/lessons/1-text-to-tensors/1.7-entropy-perplexity/) introduced alongside bits (which use base 2).

## What does a loss of 2.0 nats mean over a 248,320-way vocabulary?

Now scale it. In Qwen3.8-27B the classes are vocabulary entries, and there are 248,320 of them (Qwen3.8-27B Model Card, 2026). Every token position is a 248,320-way classification. The final linear layer that produces those logits is a matrix mapping the 5120-dimensional hidden state to 248,320 outputs: about 1.271 billion parameters. It is held separately from the embedding table (the input lookup that turns token ids into vectors) because this model does not tie the two.

Work out what the numbers mean, step by step. First establish the baseline. A model that has learned nothing spreads probability uniformly, giving every entry $1/248{,}320$, so its loss is $-\log(1/248{,}320)=\ln(248{,}320)\approx12.42$ nats per token. That is the uniform baseline, the cost of pure ignorance. It is not a ceiling, because assigning the observed token still less probability produces arbitrarily larger loss.

Now suppose training brings the average loss to 2.0 nats. Exponentiate to get perplexity: $e^{2.0}\approx7.4$. Read that as an effective branching factor: on average the model behaves as though it were choosing uniformly among about seven or eight candidates rather than a quarter of a million. The 12.42 nats of raw uncertainty have been cut to 2.0.

Keep going, because the geometry of nats is not linear. Reaching 1.9 nats sounds like a rounding difference. In perplexity it is $e^{1.9}\approx6.7$, a reduction of nearly ten percent in effective candidates. Each nat removed divides the branching factor by $e\approx2.72$. This is why loss curves that look flat late in training still represent real progress, and equally why a tenth of a nat is worth arguing about.

:::insight
Cross-entropy over 248,320 classes is what training a language model *is*. Every claim in this course about capability, scaling, or architecture ultimately reduces to whether a change moves that average negative log-probability. The model is never told which continuation is true, only how much probability it placed on the one that occurred.
:::

## When can two losses be compared?

Here is the classic wrong turn, and it survives into published comparisons. Two models report average cross-entropy of 2.0 and 2.3 nats. It is tempting to conclude the first is better. The two numbers may not be comparable at all. If the second model uses a coarser tokenizer, its tokens carry more text each, so it makes fewer, harder predictions over the same document. Loss per token is not loss per unit of text. The correction is to normalize by something tokenizer-independent, such as bits per byte or bits per character, as lesson 1.7 set up, before comparing across vocabularies. Within one tokenizer, loss per token is exactly the right currency; across tokenizers, it is a category error.

## How do implementations keep the loss numerically stable?

Numerical stability is the other place implementations go wrong. Exponentiating large logits **overflows** (the result is too large for the number format), and tiny softmax probabilities **underflow** to zero before a logarithm can be taken. Libraries therefore fuse log-softmax with negative log-likelihood using the **log-sum-exp** identity, which computes the logarithm of a sum of exponentials stably, usually by subtracting the largest logit first so that no exponential can overflow.

Two failure modes follow from ignoring this. The first is passing already-softmaxed probabilities into a primitive that expects logits, which silently applies softmax twice and flattens the distribution. The second is taking the log of a materialized zero, which yields an infinity that propagates through the whole batch.

## What does a low loss not prove?

:::warning
The most common misconception is that low loss means truth. Cross-entropy measures only how well predicted probabilities fit observed tokens in the training or evaluation corpus. A model can reach excellent loss by faithfully reproducing confident nonsense that appears in its data, and a model with slightly worse loss can be more useful, safer, or more factual. Loss is the training signal, not the definition of quality. Every claim about factuality, helpfulness, or reasoning needs its own evidence, which is what tracks 6 and beyond are for.
:::

## Which implementation details change the objective?

Two details of reduction deserve care because they change the objective without raising any error. First, batch loss is a mean or sum over examples and, for language models, over token positions. Padded positions (filler added so sequences in a batch share one length) and ignored labels need a mask, so they contribute neither to the numerator nor the denominator. Second, averaging per token weights every token equally, while averaging per sequence weights every sequence equally. Those are different objectives, and the difference is visible whenever lengths vary.

Soft targets change the problem again. With a target distribution $q$ the loss becomes $L=-\sum_i q_i\log p_i$. That is how label smoothing (moving a little target probability from the correct class to the others) and knowledge distillation (training a model to match another model's probabilities) work. These are modelling decisions, not numerical conveniences.

A practical audit uses logits you can compute by hand. Verify that raising the correct-class logit lowers the loss, that shifting all logits by one constant changes nothing, that ignored positions contribute nothing, and that the reported mean uses the denominator you intended. Those four invariants catch double-softmax, off-by-one label shifts, and padding bugs that a long, smoothly decreasing curve will otherwise hide for days.
