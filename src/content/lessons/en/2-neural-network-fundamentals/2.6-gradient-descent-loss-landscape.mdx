Backpropagation, the backward sweep of [lesson 2.5](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.5-backpropagation/), hands you a direction. It does not tell you how far to walk. That single missing number, the step size, is responsible for more failed training runs than any architectural choice. Understanding why requires looking at the shape of the function being descended rather than at the algorithm.

## What does one gradient-descent step do?

Training defines a loss $L(\theta)$ over the parameter vector $\theta$, the list of every trainable number in the model. Basic gradient descent computes the gradient $g=\nabla L(\theta)$ (the vector of slopes, one per parameter) and updates

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta-\eta g,
$$

where $\eta$ is the **learning rate**. The negative gradient is the steepest downhill direction, measured with ordinary straight-line (Euclidean) distance. That direction is strictly local. It comes from a linear approximation of the loss around the current point, and the approximation degrades as the step grows, so nothing guarantees the loss decreases for an arbitrary $\eta$.

## A ravine you can solve on paper

Watch the problem appear in a function you can solve on paper. Let

$$
L(x,y)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(10x^{2}+0.1y^{2}\right),
$$

a bowl that is a hundred times steeper along $x$ than along $y$. The gradient is $(10x,\,0.1y)$, so one step of descent gives $x\leftarrow(1-10\eta)x$ and $y\leftarrow(1-0.1\eta)y$. Each coordinate is multiplied by its own factor every step, and the whole story is in those two factors.

Try $\eta=0.15$. The $x$ factor is $1-1.5=-0.5$. The sign flips every step, so the current point (the **iterate**) bounces across the valley floor, though the magnitude halves each time and it does converge. The $y$ factor is $1-0.015=0.985$. After a hundred steps $y$ has shrunk only to $0.985^{100}\approx0.22$, and reaching one percent of its starting value takes over three hundred steps. So the shallow direction crawls.

## Why doesn't a bigger learning rate fix it?

The natural reaction is the classic wrong turn: progress along $y$ is too slow, therefore raise the learning rate. Try $\eta=0.25$. Now the $y$ factor improves to $0.975$, which is what you wanted. But the $x$ factor becomes $1-2.5=-1.5$, whose magnitude exceeds one, so $x$ grows by half every step with alternating sign and the run diverges.

The divergence threshold here is exactly $\eta=0.2$, set entirely by the steep direction, while the shallow direction would happily accept a rate a hundred times larger. One global step size cannot serve both. The gap between them is the **curvature** ratio. Curvature measures how fast the slope itself changes along a direction: the steep wall of this bowl has high curvature, the long floor low curvature. The ratio is a hundred in this toy, and vastly larger in a real network.

:::insight
This is the entire motivation for [lesson 2.7](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.7-optimizers/). Momentum, Adam, and AdamW are not arbitrary refinements. Each is an attempt to let different directions travel at different effective speeds without ever forming the second-derivative matrix that would describe the curvature exactly (for 27 billion parameters, that matrix would have 27 billion squared entries). Once you have seen a two-parameter ravine defeat a single learning rate, every optimizer that follows reads as a response to it.
:::

## Why is the gradient you see only an estimate?

Real training adds a second complication: you never see the true gradient. **Full-batch** descent averages the loss over the complete training set before each update. That is exact for that dataset, and unaffordable. **Stochastic** gradient descent uses one example. Modern practice sits between: a **minibatch** large enough for efficient matrix operations and a usable estimate, far smaller than the dataset. Sampling, shuffling, and distributed batch assembly become part of the algorithm rather than plumbing around it.

Minibatch gradients are noisy estimates. Larger batches reduce sampling variance but cost more per update. They also change optimization and generalization behavior in ways that do not follow a simple rule: doubling the batch does not license doubling the learning rate in every training setup. Effective batch size also includes gradient accumulation steps (several small batches summed before one update) and data-parallel workers (machines each processing part of the batch), so two runs described as "batch 512" may differ. Comparisons should match examples processed, update count, and compute, not merely epochs (full passes over the dataset).

## What can a loss-landscape picture show?

Now the tie to scale. For Qwen3.8-27B, $\theta$ is a vector with roughly 27 billion coordinates (Qwen3.8-27B Model Card, 2026). Every picture you have seen of a loss landscape, the smooth bowl or the dramatic ridged surface, is a two-dimensional slice or projection through a space of that dimension. It is chosen after the fact, usually along directions that make the story legible. Such plots are honest illustrations and dishonest evidence. Nothing about a 27-billion-dimensional geometry can be read off a surface rendered in three.

:::warning
The most common misconception is that a zero gradient means you have found a minimum. In high dimensions it usually means nothing of the sort. It can be a local minimum, a maximum, a **saddle point** (downhill in some directions and uphill in others), or a flat plateau, and saddles vastly outnumber minima as dimension grows. Parameter symmetries make it worse: permuting hidden units can leave the function completely unchanged, so two parameter vectors far apart in Euclidean distance may implement identical models. Judge progress by loss and downstream evaluation, never by distance travelled in parameter space or by the norm of the gradient alone.
:::

## What should you measure during training?

What you can trust is instrumentation. Plot training loss and validation loss (measured on held-out data the model never trains on) against updates *and* against tokens or examples processed, since those axes disagree whenever batch size changes. Watch gradient norms, the learning-rate schedule, overflow events in reduced precision, and throughput (examples or tokens processed per second). A jagged per-batch loss can coexist with a healthy trend; a beautifully smooth training curve can coexist with overfitting (fitting the training data at the expense of new data, the subject of [lesson 2.9](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.9-overfitting-regularization-bitter-lesson/)). Repeated runs with different random seeds tell you whether an apparent improvement clears optimization variance.

And when a run does diverge, preserve the first bad step rather than the wreckage. Record the batch identity, loss scale, gradient norm, optimizer state, and parameter statistics immediately before and after it. The earliest non-finite value names the failing operation; the cascade of not-a-numbers observed many updates later names nothing at all.
