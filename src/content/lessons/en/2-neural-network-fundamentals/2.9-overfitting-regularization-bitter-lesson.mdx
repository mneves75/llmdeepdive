A model reaches a training loss of 0.05 while a colleague's reaches 0.40. Which is better? The honest answer is that the question is unanswerable as posed, and the reason is the subject of this lesson.

## What does it mean for a model to generalize?

A model **generalizes** when patterns learned from training data support performance on new examples drawn from the distribution it will actually face. **Overfitting** is the gap between fitting the observed sample and performing on representative unseen cases. It is not the same thing as having many parameters, and a low training loss does not diagnose it. A small model can overfit badly; a very large one can generalize well. What settles it is evidence from data the model did not train on.

## How do training, validation, and test sets keep the evidence honest?

That evidence has to be structured deliberately. The **training set** updates parameters. A **validation set** guides architecture, hyperparameters, stopping point, and thresholds. A **test set** estimates the finally selected system. The rule that people break is the last one. Check the test set repeatedly, adapt to what you see, and it has become a validation set without anyone deciding so, so the number you report is optimistic by an unknown amount. Worse and more common, near-duplicate documents or temporally leaked examples (data from after the period you claim to predict) can corrupt every split while the splitting code remains formally correct.

Learning curves, which plot loss as training proceeds, are how the gap becomes visible. If training and validation losses are both high, the model may be underfitting (too weak to fit even the training data) or the optimization may be broken. If training loss falls while validation loss rises, further fitting is now harming performance under that validation distribution. Curves need uncertainty across seeds (repeat runs with different random starting values) and a meaningful horizontal axis: tokens or examples processed, not epochs (full passes over the dataset), when batch sizes differ between the runs being compared.

## What does each regularizer assume?

A **regularizer** is any choice that discourages fitting the sample at the cost of new data. The catalogue is short and each entry encodes an assumption. **Weight decay** discourages large parameters, as [lesson 2.7](/lessons/2-neural-network-fundamentals/2.7-optimizers/) detailed. **Dropout** randomly masks activations during training, so the network cannot rely on one exact co-adaptation (units that only work in a specific combination). **Data augmentation** injects label-preserving variation, such as a flipped or cropped photo that keeps its label. It is valid only when the transformation really does preserve the label, since an invalid augmentation teaches a wrong invariance with full confidence. **Early stopping** picks a checkpoint before validation performance degrades. Label smoothing, parameter sharing, and using a smaller model belong on the same list.

At large scale, the dataset itself becomes part of the regularization design. More representative coverage can reduce sampling error, diversity can expose shortcuts that fit only a narrow slice, and deduplication can limit the influence of repeated examples. None of that makes data a universally dominant regularizer or makes dropout, weight decay, and early stopping obsolete. Their relative value depends on the data distribution, objective, architecture, optimization, and evaluation evidence. Treat each as an empirical choice to ablate (remove in a controlled experiment to measure its effect, as [lesson 0.8](/lessons/0-orientation-and-foundations/0.8-how-to-read-ml-paper/) describes), not a hierarchy that holds across every training setup.

## What does the bitter lesson claim?

:::insight
This is the practical face of the **bitter lesson**. Sutton's historical argument is that AI methods leveraging growing computation through general learning and search have repeatedly overtaken systems built from elaborate hand-coded domain structure. This observation does not establish that compute or data scale replaces regularization; that tradeoff remains an empirical property of a particular training setup. Read the lesson carefully, though. It is a claim about where effort has historically paid off, not a theorem that larger always wins. Nor is it permission to ignore algorithmic efficiency, data governance, evaluation, or safety: every one of them becomes more consequential as compute magnifies the effect of your objective and your corpus.
:::

Modern deep learning also complicates the old story that capacity causes overfitting. Heavily overparameterized networks, with more parameters than training examples, can **interpolate** their training data (fit every example exactly) and still generalize. In those settings, implicit biases of the optimizer, the architecture, and the structure of the data do the work that explicit regularization used to. Parameter count on its own is therefore a poor diagnosis. The evidence comes from empirical curves, ablations, contamination checks (does the test data appear in the training data?), and out-of-distribution tests on inputs unlike the training data.

## What is not known about this course's model?

:::warning
And here the discipline turns on our own specimen. This course studies Qwen3.8-27B in detail: the layer count, the widths, the vocabulary size, and the layout are all verifiable from published configuration. Its training data is not. There is no technical report for this model, and neither the model card nor any other published source gives its training token count, dataset composition, knowledge cutoff, or training compute (Qwen3.8-27B Model Card, 2026). The lesson therefore makes no claim that one regularizer dominated this model's training and no claim about what this particular model consumed. Nobody outside the lab knows. The most common failure in reading about frontier models is treating a plausible inference as a published fact. The correct move is to state what the source supports, name what it does not, and refuse to fill the gap. You will see benchmark numbers quoted for this model throughout the course, always labeled vendor-reported, for exactly the same reason.
:::

## What does a working discipline look like?

The working discipline is unglamorous. Define the population you actually intend to serve before celebrating a training curve. Keep a genuinely held-out path and record every selection decision made against validation. Evaluate the slices where harm or failure concentrates rather than the aggregate alone. Use regularization to encode assumptions you can defend rather than as a bag of switches. Prefer scalable learning methods, while remembering that compute magnifies the consequences of your data and your objective in both directions. Generalization is demonstrated on new evidence and never by the beauty of a loss curve.

One last practical note: when the future distribution drifts over time, add a time-based holdout alongside the random split. It exposes dependence on stale templates and repeated entities that random shuffling hides completely.
