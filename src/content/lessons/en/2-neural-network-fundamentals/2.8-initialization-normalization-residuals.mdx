Here is a question with an alarming answer. Qwen3.8-27B stacks 64 layers, each carrying a 5120-dimensional hidden state per token (Qwen3.8-27B Model Card, 2026). Suppose each layer, on average, multiplies the scale of what passes through it by 1.1: a ten percent amplification, which sounds harmless. After 64 layers the signal has been multiplied by $1.1^{64}\approx446$. Now suppose the factor is 0.9 instead: $0.9^{64}\approx0.0012$, and the signal is gone.

Depth compounds. A network is not forgiving of small scale errors; it raises them to the power of its own depth. That happens in the forward pass and again in the backward pass, where the same products govern whether a gradient reaches layer one at all.

Three mechanisms manage this, each attacking a different part of the problem: initialization sets the starting scale, normalization controls the evolving scale, and residual connections shorten the path.

## How does initialization set the starting scale?

Initialization comes first, because it must do two jobs at once. First, it has to break symmetry: if every unit in a layer starts identical, they receive identical gradients and remain duplicates forever. Second, it has to control **variance**, the typical squared spread of the values around their mean. Variance-aware schemes scale random weights by **fan-in**, the number of incoming connections, and sometimes by fan-out, the number of outgoing ones. Xavier/Glorot scaling suits activations symmetric around zero, such as tanh; He-style scaling compensates for ReLU discarding roughly half the signal. These are starting assumptions, not guarantees, because gating, residual depth, and numeric precision all disturb how the scale propagates. Biases usually start at zero, since randomness in the weights already breaks symmetry.

## What does a residual connection change?

Now the structural idea that made real depth possible. A **residual connection** computes

$$
y=x+F(x)
$$

when shapes match. Read it as a claim about responsibility: the block's job is to compute a *correction* to be added to what already exists, not to reconstruct the representation from scratch. If $F$ contributes nothing useful, information still flows through the identity path untouched.

The backward pass benefits too. Each layer has a **Jacobian**, the matrix of how each of its outputs changes with each of its inputs, and without a shortcut a gradient must be multiplied by every layer's Jacobian on its way down. With $y=x+F(x)$, the derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$ contains an additive identity term. So gradient reaches earlier layers without having to survive the product of every intervening Jacobian.

This creates what practitioners call the **residual stream**: one 5120-wide vector per token that enters at the embedding (the vector looked up for each token id), travels the full depth of the model, and is written to by every sublayer along the way. Each attention block (the part that moves information between token positions) or feed-forward block reads from it, computes something, and adds the result back. Nothing overwrites it. The stream is the model's working memory, and the 64 layers are 128 successive contributions to it.

## Why does the stream still need normalization?

That raises the scale question again, in a sharper form. Suppose a fresh block adds a contribution of comparable magnitude to the stream at every step, and the contributions are roughly independent. Then the stream's variance accumulates additively. Its root-mean-square (RMS) magnitude, the square root of the average squared entry, grows like $\sqrt{\text{depth}}$, so by layer 64 it is around eight times what it was at layer one. Sublayers deep in the stack would receive inputs an order of magnitude larger than sublayers near the input. Every weight matrix would have to cope with wildly different operating scales depending on where it happened to sit.

**Normalization** is the answer, and its placement is the whole design. A **pre-norm** block computes

$$
y=x+F(\operatorname{Norm}(x)),
$$

which is what Qwen3.8-27B uses (Qwen3.8-27B Model Card, 2026). The normalization sits on the branch, not on the stream. Each sublayer receives a rescaled copy of the hidden state, always at a familiar magnitude, whether it sits at layer 3 or layer 61. Meanwhile the stream itself continues down the identity path with no operation applied to it at all.

## What does RMSNorm compute?

The specific normalizer is **RMSNorm**. For a hidden vector $x$ with $d$ entries it computes

$$
\operatorname{RMSNorm}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_i^{2}+\varepsilon}}\odot g,
$$

with a learned per-feature gain $g$ and a small stabiliser $\varepsilon$, conventionally around $10^{-6}$. Walk one vector through. Say a token's hidden state at layer 40 has a root-mean-square value of 12.0 across its 5120 entries. The denominator is $\sqrt{144+10^{-6}}=12.0$ to any precision that matters, so every entry is divided by 12 and the normalized vector has unit RMS. Take $g=1$ for this worked case; a learned gain then rescales each feature, so the sublayer sees unit RMS only where $g$ is 1.

Now consider a near-dead position whose entries are all around $10^{-5}$. The mean square is $10^{-10}$, which is ten thousand times smaller than $\varepsilon$. So the epsilon dominates the square root, and the output is scaled down toward zero rather than exploding through a division by almost nothing. That is precisely the job epsilon does: it is the guard that keeps the operation defined at the bottom of the range, not a rounding fudge.

Note what RMSNorm does *not* do: it never subtracts the mean. **LayerNorm**, the older normalizer, centers the feature vector (subtracts its mean) before scaling; RMSNorm only rescales.

:::caveat
It is tempting to describe RMSNorm as "LayerNorm but faster". That understates the change. Removing mean subtraction changes the mathematics as well as the kernel: the two operations produce different functions of the same input, and a model trained under one is not interchangeable with a model trained under the other. Speed also depends on kernels, fusion, and hardware rather than on the formula alone. Whether the trade is worthwhile is an empirical question at the architecture level, and the modern answer, visible in this course's specimen, is that it is.
:::

## Why does the norm go on the branch?

The classic wrong turn here is placement. The original Transformer used **post-norm**, $y=\operatorname{Norm}(x+F(x))$, which puts a normalization operation *on the stream itself*. It works, and at moderate depth it works well. But it destroys the clean identity path. Gradient flowing backward must now pass through a normalization at every one of the 64 steps, and the deep stacks that result are notoriously fragile to train, needing careful warmup (small learning rates for the first steps) and sometimes failing outright. Moving the norm onto the branch, the one-line change from post-norm to pre-norm, restores the unobstructed additive highway. That is why pre-norm is the default in essentially every large model shipped since.

:::warning
The most common misconception is that pre-norm keeps the residual stream at unit scale. It does not, and cannot: nothing in $x+F(\operatorname{Norm}(x))$ ever rescales $x$. The stream grows with depth exactly as described above; what pre-norm guarantees is that no *sublayer* ever has to deal with that growth. This has a visible consequence you can check in any checkpoint. A final normalization sits after the last layer, before the output head, because the raw stream arriving at layer 64 is at the wrong scale for a projection into vocabulary logits.
:::

## How do you check a deep stack before training?

These mechanisms are infrastructure, and it matters that you read them as such. None of them decides what the model learns. They make deep composition numerically survivable, so that the attention and feed-forward blocks of track 4 can spend the optimization budget learning useful transformations rather than fighting collapse. [Lesson 4.10](/lessons/4-transformer/4.10-residuals-pre-norm-post-norm/) returns to this arrangement with the full transformer block in view.

An empirical check is cheap and worth building early. Before training, push representative inputs through the model and record per-layer activation means, root-mean-square values, and gradient norms after one loss. Look for monotonic collapse, explosive growth, dead branches, and discontinuities at residual additions. Run it in the precision you will actually train in, since bf16 rounding (a 16-bit floating-point format common in training) is part of the system you are testing.
