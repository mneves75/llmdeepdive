Vector databases and HNSW from [Vector Databases, HNSW & Filtered ANN Search](/lessons/27-retrieval-and-hybrid-search/27.4-ann-vector-indexes-and-hnsw/) excel at paraphrases (different words expressing the same idea): search `"recover locked two-factor account"` and dense search pulls `"MFA (multi-factor authentication) reset procedure"` cleanly. Now search for an exact identifier—`ERR_SSL_VERSION_OR_CIPHER_MISMATCH`, invoice `INV-2026-0419`, or Stock Keeping Unit (SKU) part code `SKU-9482-B`—and dense embeddings routinely miss the exact document sitting right in front of them. Conversely, lexical (exact word-for-word) search nails exact identifiers and misses paraphrases. **Hybrid search** runs both retrievers in parallel (side by side at the same time) and fuses their rankings.

## Okapi BM25: exact lexical matching with saturation

The standard lexical retriever is **Okapi BM25** (Best Matching 25), formalized in Robertson and Zaragoza (2009). Rather than counting raw keyword hits—which keyword-stuffed or sprawling documents would game—BM25 weights every matching query term $t$ using three ideas:

1. **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF):** rare words across the corpus (the full collection of documents) carry more signal than common words like `"system"`.
2. **Term-frequency saturation ($k_1$, standard default `1.5`):** seeing a term 3 times matters far more than seeing it once, yet seeing it 30 times is not 10× more relevant than 3—the score grows more slowly and levels off at a ceiling.
3. **Document length normalization ($b$, standard default `0.75`):** a 400-token chunk mentioning a word 3 times is less concentrated than a 250-token average chunk mentioning it 3 times.

Walk through a concrete single-term calculation before looking at the general equations. Suppose a corpus has $N = 10{,}000$ documents, the query term appears in $\text{df} = 250$ documents (document frequency), and a candidate document of length $\text{docLen} = 400$ tokens (against corpus average $\text{avgDocLen} = 250$) contains the term $\text{tf} = 3$ times (term frequency), with $k_1 = 1.5$ and $b = 0.75$:

- **Smoothed IDF** (adjusted with `+ 1` and `+ 0.5` inside the natural logarithm so scores stay positive and never divide by zero): $\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{10{,}000 - 250 + 0.5}{250 + 0.5}\right) = \ln(1 + 38.924) = 3.687$.
- **Length normalization factor:** $1 - 0.75 + 0.75 \cdot \frac{400}{250} = 0.25 + 1.20 = 1.45$.
- **BM25 term score:** $3.687 \cdot \frac{3 \cdot (1.5 + 1)}{3 + 1.5 \cdot 1.45} = 3.687 \cdot \frac{7.5}{5.175} = 5.343$.

In general, using the standard Lucene/Robertson–Spärck Jones smoothed IDF so scores stay non-negative:

$$\text{IDF}(t) = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{N - \text{df}(t) + 0.5}{\text{df}(t) + 0.5}\right)$$

$$\text{BM25}(t, d) = \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{\text{tf} \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{\text{tf} + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|d|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$

For a multi-word query, BM25 sums this score across every query term present in document $d$. Notice that as $\text{tf} \to \infty$, the fraction saturates at $k_1 + 1 = 2.5$, bounding any single term's contribution below $3.687 \times 2.5 = 9.22$ no matter how heavily a document repeats the word.

## Why raw score blending is brittle

Once you have a dense top-50 list (cosine scores in `[-1, 1]`, often clustered tightly between `0.72` and `0.86`) and a BM25 top-50 list (unbounded scores in `[0, ∞)` that shift with query length and corpus IDF), a tempting shortcut is linear blending (multiplying each score by a fixed weight and adding them): $\alpha \cdot s_{\text{dense}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot s_{\text{BM25}}$.

That shortcut breaks quickly in production:

- **Incompatible scales:** adding `0.82` cosine to `14.6` BM25 lets BM25 dominate unless you rescale every query.
- **Min-max distortion:** rescaling a query's top-50 BM25 scores into `[0, 1]` (stretching the lowest score to 0 and the highest to 1) turns an irrelevant outlier (`0.4` vs `0.1`) into a fake `1.0`, promoting noise whenever lexical search had no real match.

## Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

Cormack, Clarke, and Büttcher (2009) showed that discarding raw scores altogether and combining **rank positions** via **Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)** beats complex score-calibration heuristics across diverse corpora. For each candidate document $d$ across retrievers $r \in R$ (using 1-based rank $\text{rank}_r(d)$—where first place is 1, second is 2—and omitting retrievers that did not return $d$):

$$\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in R} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}$$

The smoothing constant $k = 60$ damps the gap between rank #1 and rank #3 so neither retriever's isolated #1 outlier overwhelms strong agreement from both engines.

Compute the worked comparison with $k = 60$ across three candidate documents:

- **Document A** (dense #1, BM25 #40): $\frac{1}{60 + 1} + \frac{1}{60 + 40} = 0.01639 + 0.01000 = 0.02639$.
- **Document B** (dense #3, BM25 #2): $\frac{1}{60 + 3} + \frac{1}{60 + 2} = 0.01587 + 0.01613 = 0.03200$ (**wins**).
- **Document C** (dense #2, absent from BM25): $\frac{1}{60 + 2} = 0.01613$.

Document B finishes decisively ahead of Document A—even though Document A was dense #1—because both semantic and lexical search place Document B near the top. Document C drops to third because only one retriever backed it.

:::insight
RRF needs zero training data, zero per-query score normalization, and extends directly to three or more lists (for example, dense + BM25 + metadata-boosted title index). Start every production Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system with hybrid BM25 + dense + RRF before tuning custom weights.
:::

## Limits of rank-only fusion

RRF has one real blind spot: because it discards raw score margins, it treats a massive confidence lead the same as a photo-finish tie. If an exact SKU query yields a BM25 #1 score of `28.0` and #2 score of `1.1`, while dense search returns irrelevant guesses, unweighted RRF still gives dense #1 equal rank weight (`1/61`). Two lightweight fixes handle that edge case cleanly in Track 28: adding modest modality weights (favoring keyword or vector search slightly when domain evaluation sets justify it), or—far better—passing the fused top-50 candidates to a **cross-encoder reranker** (a second-stage model that reads the query and each candidate chunk together to judge relevance directly).
